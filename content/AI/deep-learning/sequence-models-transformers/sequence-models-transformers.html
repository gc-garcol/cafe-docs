Sequence models and transformers

Prices, order books and text arrive as sequences, where order matters and the clue may be far back. Recurrent networks carry a memory forward one step at a time. Transformers let every position look at every other position directly, through attention.

1. Why sequences are different

  
A tabular model sees a fixed list of features. A sequence model sees $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_T$, where $T$ can vary, the order carries meaning, and the relevant clue for $x_T$ might be one step back or five hundred. Market data is full of this:

  
    
    
  • Tick and bar streams: returns, volumes and spreads, where volatility clusters over hours and days.
    • 
    
  • Limit order books: each event changes the book, and the next mid-price move depends on recent order flow.
    • 
    
  • Text: earnings calls, filings and news, where "it" in one sentence refers to a company named three sentences earlier.
    • 
  

  
The standard training task is next-step prediction. Given the past, predict the next value or token, and minimize the error over every position:

  
$$\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta\bigl(x_{t+1} \mid x_1, \dots, x_t\bigr)$$

  
Any model for this must be causal: its prediction at $t$ may only use positions up to $t$. That one rule is what the causal mask in Fig 1 enforces.






  
2. Recurrent networks

  
A recurrent neural network (RNN) reads one step at a time and keeps a hidden state $h_t$, a summary of everything seen so far:

  
$$h_t = \tanh\bigl(W h_{t-1} + U x_t + b\bigr), \qquad \hat y_t = V h_t$$

  
The same weights $W$, $U$, $V$ are used at every step, so the model works for any length. Training unrolls the loop and runs backpropagation through time. The gradient of a late loss with respect to an early state is a product of one Jacobian per step:

  
$$\frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_t} = \prod_{s=t+1}^{T} \operatorname{diag}\bigl(1 - h_s^2\bigr)\, W \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \left\lVert \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_t} \right\rVert \lesssim \bigl(\gamma\,\rho(W)\bigr)^{T-t}$$

  
Here $\rho(W)$ is the largest singular value of $W$ and $\gamma \le 1$ bounds the tanh slope. If $\gamma\rho \lt 1$ the gradient shrinks exponentially with distance, so the network cannot learn long-range links: vanishing gradients. If it is above 1 the gradient can blow up instead. Fig 2 shows how fast this happens.






  
3. LSTM and GRU: an additive memory

  
The long short-term memory (LSTM) cell adds a second state $c_t$ that is updated by addition, not by a matrix multiply. Gates between 0 and 1 decide what to forget, what to write and what to show:

  
$$\begin{aligned} f_t &= \sigma(W_f [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f) && \text{forget} \\ i_t &= \sigma(W_i [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_i) && \text{input} \\ o_t &= \sigma(W_o [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_o) && \text{output} \\ c_t &= f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tanh(W_c [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_c) \\ h_t &= o_t \odot \tanh(c_t) \end{aligned}$$

  
Along the cell state, the gradient is just the product of forget gates, $\prod_s f_s$. When the network learns to keep $f$ near 1, information and gradients survive for hundreds of steps. That is why initializing the forget-gate bias to about 1 helps. The GRU merges the cell and hidden state and uses two gates instead of three. It is a little cheaper and usually about as good.

4. Attention

  
Attention drops the step-by-step chain. Each position $i$ builds three vectors from its input: a query $q_i$ ("what am I looking for?"), a key $k_j$ ("what do I contain?") and a value $v_j$ ("what do I pass on?"). Position $i$ scores every key, turns the scores into weights with a softmax, and takes the weighted average of the values:

  
$$\operatorname{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\right) V, \qquad M_{ij} = \begin{cases} 0 & j \le i \\ -\infty & j > i \ \ (\text{causal}) \end{cases}$$

  
With $Q = XW_Q$, $K = XW_K$ and $V = XW_V$ for learned matrices. The division by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps the scores in a sensible range: if the entries of $q$ and $k$ have unit variance, their dot product over $d_k$ dimensions has variance $d_k$, and without the scaling the softmax would saturate to a single winner with tiny gradients. That is the Sharpness slider in Fig 1.

  
Multi-head attention runs $h$ such heads in parallel on smaller projections, $d_k = d/h$, and concatenates the results. One head can track syntax, another recency, another a specific entity. The path from any position to any other is a single step, so long-range gradients do not vanish the way they do in an RNN.






  
5. The transformer block

  
A transformer stacks identical blocks. Each block has an attention sub-layer and a small two-layer network applied to every position separately, each wrapped in a residual connection and layer normalization:

  
$$\begin{aligned} z &= x + \operatorname{MultiHead}\bigl(\operatorname{LN}(x)\bigr) \\ x' &= z + W_2\,\phi\bigl(W_1 \operatorname{LN}(z)\bigr) \end{aligned}$$

  
Attention by itself ignores order: shuffle the inputs and the outputs shuffle with them. Position must be added explicitly. The original transformer added sinusoids of different frequencies to the inputs:

  
$$PE_{(p,\,2i)} = \sin\!\left(\frac{p}{10000^{2i/d}}\right), \qquad PE_{(p,\,2i+1)} = \cos\!\left(\frac{p}{10000^{2i/d}}\right)$$

  
Modern models more often use learned position embeddings or rotary embeddings (RoPE), which rotate $q$ and $k$ by an angle proportional to position, so their dot product depends on the relative distance $i - j$.

Attention trains in parallel over the whole sequence, which is why it scales to huge data sets on GPUs. The price is the $T^2$ term: a sequence of 10,000 order book events needs a 100-million-entry attention matrix per head. Sparse, windowed and linear-attention variants, and state-space models such as Mamba, attack exactly that cost.

  
Dilated causal convolutions are the cheap alternative for numeric series. With kernel size $k$ and dilations $1, 2, 4, \dots, 2^{L-1}$, the receptive field grows exponentially with depth:

  
$$\text{receptive field} = 1 + (k-1)\sum_{l=0}^{L-1} 2^l = 1 + (k-1)(2^L - 1)$$






  
6. Sequence models in markets

  
The architecture is rarely the bottleneck in finance. The data is.

  
    
    
  • Signal-to-noise is tiny. A daily return forecast with a correlation of 0.05 is good. A model with millions of parameters will happily fit the noise. Strong regularization, early stopping and small models win more often than big ones.
    • 
    
  • The process changes. Patterns from 2015 may not hold in 2025. Train and test with walk-forward and purged cross-validation, never with shuffled windows.
    • 
    
  • Inputs need care. Feed returns, volatility-scaled values or ranks, not raw prices, and normalize with past data only. See feature engineering and leakage.
    • 
    
  • Where it works best: rich, high-frequency inputs. Models such as DeepLOB combine convolutions over the order book with an LSTM over time to predict short-term mid-price moves. Transformers over news and filings produce sentiment and event features. Pretrained time-series transformers give reasonable zero-shot forecasts for smooth business series, less so for returns.
    • 
  

  
Beat a linear model on lagged features first. If the transformer cannot, the problem is the data, not the depth.






  
7. A minimal causal attention layer

  
import torch, torch.nn as nn, math

class CausalSelfAttention(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, heads):
        super().__init__()
        self.h, self.dk = heads, d // heads
        self.qkv = nn.Linear(d, 3 * d)
        self.out = nn.Linear(d, d)

    def forward(self, x):                      # x: (batch, T, d)
        B, T, d = x.shape
        q, k, v = self.qkv(x).split(d, dim=-1)
        q, k, v = (t.view(B, T, self.h, self.dk).transpose(1, 2) for t in (q, k, v))
        scores = q @ k.transpose(-2, -1) / math.sqrt(self.dk)
        mask = torch.triu(torch.ones(T, T, dtype=torch.bool), diagonal=1)
        scores = scores.masked_fill(mask, float("-inf"))    # no peeking ahead
        y = scores.softmax(dim=-1) @ v
        return self.out(y.transpose(1, 2).reshape(B, T, d))

  
In practice, torch.nn.functional.scaled_dot_product_attention(q, k, v, is_causal=True) does the same with a fused, memory-efficient kernel.






  
8. Common mistakes

  
    
    
  • Forgetting the causal mask, or using a bidirectional model for forecasting. The model reads the answer from later positions.
    • 
    
  • Normalizing each window with its own future. Scaling a window by its full mean and standard deviation leaks the last values into the first ones.
    • 
    
  • Overlapping training windows split at random. Neighboring windows share almost all their data, so validation scores are inflated.
    • 
    
  • Weak baselines. Compare with last value, a moving average, ARMA or GARCH, and a linear model on lags. Many published deep forecasts lose to these.
    • 
    
  • Ignoring the cost of $T^2$. Doubling the context quadruples attention memory. Downsample, window or summarize old history.
    • 
  






  
9. Where it shows up

  
    
    
  • Language models: every large language model is a causal transformer trained on next-token prediction.
    • 
    
  • Finance: order book models, volatility forecasting, news and earnings-call analysis, and execution agents that read recent market state. See also policy gradients for learning to act on such states.
    • 
    
  • Other domains: speech, protein structure, weather forecasting and recommendation systems that model a user's click history.
    • 
    
  • Related: convolutions share weights across positions in space, recurrent networks share them across time, and attention learns which positions to share information between.
