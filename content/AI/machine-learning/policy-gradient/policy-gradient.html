Readout

    

  






  
1. Learn the policy directly

  
Value methods such as Q-learning learn how good each action is and then pick the best. Policy gradient methods skip that step. A policy $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ with parameters $\theta$ outputs action probabilities, and training adjusts $\theta$ to raise the expected total reward $J(\theta)$.

  
This handles continuous actions and stochastic policies naturally.






  
2. REINFORCE

  
The policy gradient theorem gives a simple, sampled gradient:

  
$$\nabla_\theta J=\mathbb{E}\Big[\,G\;\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\Big]$$

  
Take an action, see the return $G$, and then push up the log-probability of that action in proportion to $G$. Good outcomes make their actions likelier, bad ones less so. For a softmax, $\nabla\log\pi$ for the chosen action is "one minus its probability" and for others minus their probabilities.

  
It is unbiased but noisy. Run it without a baseline and watch the reward curve wobble.






  
3. Baseline: less noise, same answer

  
Replace $G$ by $G-b$, where $b$ is something that does not depend on the action, usually the average return. The expected gradient does not change, but the variance drops sharply. The difference $A=G-b$ is called the advantage: how much better this action was than usual.






  
4. PPO: do not step too far

  
One bad, large update can wreck a policy, and fresh data is expensive. Proximal Policy Optimization reuses each batch for several small steps, but keeps the new policy close to the old one. With the probability ratio $r=\pi_\theta/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ it maximizes

  
$$L=\mathbb{E}\Big[\min\big(r\,A,\;\operatorname{clip}(r,\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\,A\big)\Big]$$

  
Once the ratio leaves $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$ in the direction that helps, the gradient goes flat and the update stops pushing. The picture below shows the two cases.






  
5. Where it is used

  
    
    
  • PPO is a default for robotics and games, and was a core step in training language models from human feedback (RLHF).
    • 
    
  • Actor-critic methods combine a policy (the actor) with a learned value (the critic) that serves as the baseline.
    • 
    
  • Compared with DQN, policy gradients are more stable and allow continuous actions, but need more samples.
