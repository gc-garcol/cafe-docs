Random Forest

One decision tree memorizes noise. A forest of different trees, voting together, cancels most of it.

1. Why one tree is not enough

  
A Decision Tree that grows deep enough fits its training data exactly, including the noise. Change a few points and the tree changes a lot. That instability is high variance.






  
2. Two sources of randomness

  
A random forest trains many trees that are deliberately different, then averages them.

  
    
    
  • Bagging. Each tree trains on a bootstrap sample: $n$ rows drawn with replacement. About 37% of rows are left out of any one tree, and those out-of-bag rows give a free validation estimate.
    • 
    
  • Random features. At every split only a random subset of $m$ features is considered. A strong feature cannot dominate all trees, so trees stay decorrelated. A common default is $m=\sqrt{d}$ for classification.
    • 
  






  
3. Why averaging helps

  
If $B$ trees each have variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$, their average has variance

  
$$\rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2$$

  
More trees shrink the second term toward zero. Lower correlation $\rho$ shrinks the first. That is why the random feature choice matters as much as the number of trees.






  
4. Practical notes

  
    
    
  • More trees never hurt accuracy, they only cost time. Returns flatten after a few hundred.
    • 
    
  • Deep trees are fine here, since averaging handles the variance.
    • 
    
  • Feature importance comes for free: how much each feature reduced impurity, or how much accuracy drops when it is shuffled.
    • 
    
  • It needs no feature scaling and handles mixed feature types. It is weaker than boosting on many tabular tasks and less interpretable than one tree.
    •
