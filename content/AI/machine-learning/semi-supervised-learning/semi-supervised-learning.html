Semi-Supervised Learning

Labels cost money, raw data is cheap. Use a handful of labeled examples to name the points, and the shape of the unlabeled data to spread those names.

1. The setting

  
In semi-supervised learning you have a few labeled examples and many unlabeled ones. It sits between supervised and unsupervised learning. It pays off when the unlabeled data tells you something about how the classes are shaped.

  
The key assumptions: points close together probably share a label (smoothness), and class boundaries pass through low-density gaps (cluster assumption).






  
2. Self-training

  
Train a model on the labeled data. Predict the unlabeled points, keep only the predictions the model is most sure about, treat them as true labels, retrain, and repeat. Here the model is a nearest-centroid classifier.

  
    
    
  • Simple, and works with any model.
    • 
    
  • A confident mistake becomes a training label and is reinforced. This is called confirmation bias. Try it on the moons.
    • 
  






  
3. Co-training

  
Suppose each example has two independent views, such as a web page's text and the text of links pointing to it. Train one model per view. Each model labels the points it is sure about and hands them to the other as training data. Because the views are independent, one model's confident answers are fresh information to the other.






  
4. Label propagation

  
Build a graph where each point links to its nearest neighbors, with heavier edges for closer points. Labels then flow along the edges, and each point takes a weighted mix of its neighbors' labels, while the labeled points stay pinned. With normalized weights $S$, the update is

  
$$F \leftarrow \alpha\,S\,F + (1-\alpha)\,Y$$

  
where $Y$ holds the known labels. Labels travel along the crescent, so each moon gets one color even though the nearest labeled point of the other class may be closer in a straight line.






  
5. Choosing

  
    
    
  • Self-training is a quick baseline. Watch for confirmation bias, and use a confidence threshold.
    • 
    
  • Label propagation is strong when the data lie on curved shapes, and gets expensive for large graphs.
    • 
    
  • Modern deep methods pair this with augmentation and consistency. They ask for the same prediction on two noisy copies of an input.
    •
