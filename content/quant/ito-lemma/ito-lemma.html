Itô's Lemma

In ordinary calculus a small change in $f(S)$ is $f'(S)\,dS$. When $S$ follows Brownian motion, the squared move $(dS)^2$ is as large as $dt$ and adds up to something real. Itô's lemma is the chain rule with that extra term.

Split the change of f(S) into pieces

One stock path follows geometric Brownian motion. Along it, the change of a function $f(S)$ is split into the first-order Taylor term $\sum f'(S)\,\Delta S$ and the second-order term $\sum \tfrac12 f''(S)\,(\Delta S)^2$. Compare both with the true change.

How to use this simulator

  1. Keep $f(S) = \ln S$. The blue curve, first order only, drifts away from the true $\ln S_t$ in black. The orange curve adds the second-order term and sits on top of it.
  2. Raise the volatility. The gap grows like $\tfrac12\sigma^2 t$, exactly Itô's correction for the log.
  3. Raise the number of steps. In ordinary calculus the second-order term would vanish. Here it does not, because $(\Delta S)^2$ is of size $\Delta t$.
  4. Try $f(S) = S^2$ and $f(S) = \sqrt S$. The correction changes sign with the curvature $f''$.

1. Why ordinary calculus breaks

Expand $f$ around $S$ with Taylor's theorem:

$$df = f'(S)\,dS + \tfrac12 f''(S)\,(dS)^2 + \dots$$

For a smooth path, $(dS)^2$ is of order $dt^2$ and drops out. For $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW$, the rule $(dW)^2 = dt$ from Brownian motion gives $(dS)^2 = \sigma^2 S^2\,dt$, which is first order. Summed over a year of tiny steps, it is a finite number, not zero.

2. The lemma

For $dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t$ and a twice differentiable $f(t, S)$:

$$df = \Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \mu S\frac{\partial f}{\partial S} + \frac12\sigma^2 S^2\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial S^2}\Big)dt + \sigma S\frac{\partial f}{\partial S}\,dW_t$$

Use the multiplication rules $dt\cdot dt = 0$, $dt\cdot dW = 0$ and $dW\cdot dW = dt$, and keep everything of order $dt$.

3. Example: the log price

With $f = \ln S$ we have $f' = 1/S$ and $f'' = -1/S^2$, so

$$d\ln S_t = \Big(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)dt + \sigma\,dW_t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S_t = S_0\,e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t + \sigma W_t}$$

The $-\sigma^2/2$ is the same volatility drag that makes a typical investor's growth rate lower than the mean return. In Fig 1 it is the gap between the blue and black curves.

Applied to an option price $V(t,S)$, Itô's lemma is the first step of the Black-Scholes derivation: hedge away the $dW$ term with the stock, and what remains must earn the risk-free rate.

Pitfalls

  • The Itô integral uses the value at the start of each step. Evaluating at the midpoint gives the Stratonovich integral, which follows ordinary calculus rules but a different model.
  • The correction depends on curvature. Linear functions have $f'' = 0$ and no correction.
  • "$(dW)^2 = dt$" is shorthand for a limit of sums. It is not true for a single step: one $(\Delta W)^2$ is random, only the sum converges.
  • Mean log return is $\mu - \sigma^2/2$, mean simple return is $\mu$. Mixing them is a common source of bias.
