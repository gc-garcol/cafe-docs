Mean-Variance Portfolios

Markowitz's idea: judge a portfolio by its expected return and its risk together, and only hold portfolios you cannot improve on both at once. Those form the efficient frontier, and adding a risk-free asset picks one best mix of risky assets for everyone.

1. Portfolio return and risk

With weights $w$ summing to 1, expected returns $\mu$ and covariance matrix $\Sigma$:

$$\mu_P = w^\top\mu, \qquad \sigma_P^2 = w^\top\Sigma\,w = \sum_{i,j} w_i w_j \rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j$$

Return is a weighted average, risk is not. Every pair of assets with $\rho < 1$ brings the portfolio's risk below the weighted average of the risks. That gap is diversification.

2. The efficient frontier

For each target return $m$, find the weights with the least variance:

$$\min_w\; w^\top\Sigma\,w \quad \text{subject to}\quad w^\top\mu = m,\;\; w^\top\mathbf 1 = 1$$

Without other constraints this has a closed-form solution, and the frontier is a hyperbola in the $(\sigma, \mu)$ plane. Its leftmost point is the minimum-variance portfolio $w \propto \Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1$. Only the upper half is efficient. With a no-short constraint, a quadratic program is solved instead.

3. The tangency portfolio

Add a risk-free asset with rate $r_f$. Any mix of it with a risky portfolio lies on a straight line. The steepest such line touches the frontier at the tangency portfolio, the one with the highest Sharpe ratio:

$$w_{\text{tan}} \propto \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f\mathbf 1), \qquad \text{Sharpe} = \frac{\mu_P - r_f}{\sigma_P}$$

That line is the capital market line. In theory every investor holds the same risky mix and only chooses how much of it to hold versus cash. This is the starting point of CAPM.

Sigma = np.outer(vol, vol) * corr
inv = np.linalg.inv(Sigma)
w_min = inv @ np.ones(3);  w_min /= w_min.sum()          # minimum variance
w_tan = inv @ (mu - rf);   w_tan /= w_tan.sum()          # max Sharpe, shorts allowed

Pitfalls

  • The optimizer is an error maximizer. Small errors in $\mu$ produce extreme, unstable weights. Expected returns are the hardest input to estimate.
  • Shrink the covariance matrix (Ledoit-Wolf) and the means, or use constraints, to keep weights sensible.
  • Variance punishes upside and downside alike, and ignores fat tails. Drawdown and tail risk need separate checks.
  • Correlations rise in crises, so the diversification you measured in calm times may not be there when you need it.
