ARMA and GARCH

Returns are hard to predict, but their size is not. Big moves come in clusters, and quiet days follow quiet days. ARMA models the conditional mean of a series. GARCH models its conditional variance, which is what risk and option desks care about most.

How to use this simulator

  1. Set $\alpha = 0$ and $\beta = 0$. Volatility is constant and returns look like plain noise.
  2. Press Equity-like: $\alpha = 0.08$, $\beta = 0.9$. Quiet and stormy periods appear, and squared returns become strongly autocorrelated while returns stay uncorrelated.
  3. Push $\alpha + \beta$ toward 1. Volatility shocks last longer, and the half-life in the readout grows.
  4. Give the mean an AR coefficient $\phi$. Now returns themselves have a small first-lag autocorrelation, the ARMA part.

1. ARMA: the conditional mean

An ARMA($p$, $q$) model explains today's value with past values and past shocks:

$$x_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i\,x_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t + \sum_{j=1}^{q}\theta_j\,\varepsilon_{t-j}$$

The AR part gives a slowly decaying autocorrelation, the MA part a cut-off after $q$ lags. For daily stock returns the coefficients are tiny, so ARMA is used more for spreads, rates, volumes and macro series than for raw returns.

2. GARCH: the conditional variance

GARCH(1,1) lets today's variance depend on yesterday's squared shock and yesterday's variance:

$$\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^2, \qquad \varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t,\; z_t \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$$

$\alpha$ is how strongly a surprise raises volatility, $\beta$ how long volatility remembers. With $\alpha + \beta < 1$ the variance reverts to a long-run level, and shocks fade with a half-life of $\ln 0.5 / \ln(\alpha+\beta)$ days:

$$\bar\sigma^2 = \frac{\omega}{1-\alpha-\beta}$$

Even with normal $z_t$, the mixture of calm and stormy days makes the unconditional distribution of returns fat-tailed, with excess kurtosis above 0. Typical equity estimates are $\alpha \approx 0.05$ to $0.1$ and $\beta \approx 0.9$.

3. Fitting and forecasting

GARCH parameters are fitted by maximum likelihood: each day's return is normal with the variance the model predicted the day before. The fitted model then forecasts volatility, which feeds Value at Risk, position sizing and option pricing.

$$\mathbb E_t\big[\sigma_{t+h}^2\big] = \bar\sigma^2 + (\alpha+\beta)^{h-1}\big(\sigma_{t+1}^2 - \bar\sigma^2\big)$$
from arch import arch_model
am = arch_model(100 * returns, mean="AR", lags=1, vol="GARCH", p=1, q=1, dist="t")
res = am.fit(disp="off")
print(res.params)                          # mu, phi, omega, alpha, beta, nu
f = res.forecast(horizon=10)               # variance forecasts for the next 10 days

Pitfalls

  • Plain GARCH treats up and down moves alike. Equity volatility rises more after falls; GJR-GARCH and EGARCH add that leverage effect.
  • $\alpha + \beta$ estimated very close to 1 often signals a structural break, not truly permanent shocks.
  • Residuals stay fat-tailed after GARCH. A Student t distribution for $z_t$ usually fits better.
  • Scale returns, for example to percent, before fitting. Optimizers struggle with variances near $10^{-4}$.
