Markov Chains

A Markov chain jumps between a few states, and the chance of each jump depends only on the current state. That one assumption turns a messy process into a matrix, and the matrix tells you where the process spends its time and how fast it forgets where it started.

A three-regime market

Each day the market is in one of three regimes: bull, sideways or bear. The sliders set how likely each regime is to persist for another day. The rest of the probability is split between the other two regimes.

How to use this simulator

  1. Start from "bear" and look at the top-right chart. The probability of each regime moves from $(0, 0, 1)$ to a fixed mix: the stationary distribution $\pi$.
  2. Raise all three persistence sliders to 0.97. Regimes last much longer, and the distribution takes far longer to settle. The readout shows the second eigenvalue $|\lambda_2|$ close to 1.
  3. Lower bear persistence to 0.5. Bear spells get short and $\pi$ puts less weight on bear.
  4. Press "New path" a few times and compare the time spent in each regime on the bottom strip with $\pi$. Long runs match better: that is the ergodic theorem.

1. The Markov property

A sequence of random states $X_0, X_1, X_2, \dots$ is a Markov chain if the future depends on the past only through the present:

$$P(X_{t+1}=j \mid X_t=i,\, X_{t-1},\dots,X_0) = P(X_{t+1}=j \mid X_t=i) = p_{ij}$$

The chain has no memory beyond its current state. If the probabilities $p_{ij}$ do not change over time the chain is time-homogeneous, which is the usual assumption. A random walk is a Markov chain on the integers. Brownian motion is its continuous-time, continuous-state cousin.

2. The transition matrix

Collect the probabilities in a matrix $P$. Row $i$ is the distribution of tomorrow's state given today's state $i$, so every row sums to 1. If $\pi_t$ is the row vector of state probabilities at time $t$, one step is a matrix product, and $n$ steps are a matrix power:

$$\pi_{t+1} = \pi_t P, \qquad \pi_t = \pi_0 P^t, \qquad P(X_{t+n}=j \mid X_t = i) = (P^n)_{ij}$$

The last identity is the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation: to go from $i$ to $j$ in $m+n$ steps, sum over every state you could be in after $m$ steps, $P^{m+n} = P^m P^n$.

3. The stationary distribution

A distribution $\pi$ that one step leaves unchanged is stationary:

$$\pi P = \pi, \qquad \sum_i \pi_i = 1, \quad \pi_i \ge 0$$

So $\pi$ is a left eigenvector of $P$ with eigenvalue 1. If the chain is irreducible (every state can reach every other) and aperiodic (it does not cycle on a fixed rhythm), then $\pi$ is unique and $\pi_0 P^t \to \pi$ from any start. The gap to $\pi$ shrinks roughly like $|\lambda_2|^t$, where $\lambda_2$ is the second-largest eigenvalue in modulus. Close to 1 means a slow-mixing chain with long memory.

The ergodic theorem adds that the fraction of time one long path spends in state $i$ converges to $\pi_i$. Time averages equal ensemble averages, which is what lets you estimate $\pi$ from a single history.

4. How long a regime lasts

Once in state $i$, the chain stays each further day with probability $p_{ii}$. The length of a spell is geometric:

$$P(\text{spell} = k) = p_{ii}^{\,k-1}(1-p_{ii}), \qquad \mathbb{E}[\text{spell}] = \frac{1}{1-p_{ii}}$$

A persistence of 0.95 means an average spell of 20 days, and 0.99 means 100 days. Small changes near 1 make large changes in duration, which is why regime models are sensitive to how these numbers are estimated.

5. In quant work

  • Regime models. Hidden Markov and regime-switching models assume a Markov chain like this one drives the mean and volatility of returns. You do not see the regime, only returns, and infer it with Bayes' rule.
  • Credit migration. Rating agencies publish one-year transition matrices between ratings. Powers of the matrix give multi-year default probabilities.
  • Microstructure. The sign of the next trade, or whether the next price move is up or down, is often modeled as a small Markov chain on order book states.

Estimating $P$ from an observed state sequence is maximum likelihood on counts: $\hat p_{ij} = n_{ij}/n_i$, the share of visits to $i$ that were followed by $j$.

import numpy as np
counts = np.zeros((k, k))
np.add.at(counts, (states[:-1], states[1:]), 1)
P = counts / counts.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)        # MLE of the transition matrix
w, v = np.linalg.eig(P.T)                               # left eigenvectors of P
pi = np.real(v[:, np.argmin(np.abs(w - 1))]); pi /= pi.sum()
durations = 1 / (1 - np.diag(P))                       # expected spell length per state

Pitfalls

  • The Markov property is an assumption. Volatility, for example, has long memory, so a chain on "high or low vol" days fits worse than its simplicity suggests. Check it by testing whether two-day history predicts better than one day.
  • Rare transitions are estimated from very few counts. A matrix fitted on ten years of data may see only a handful of bear-to-bull switches, so report uncertainty, for example by bootstrapping whole spells.
  • Transition probabilities drift. A matrix estimated in one decade can be wrong in the next, so re-estimate on rolling windows.
  • Defining regimes after the fact, using future data, leaks information into any strategy that conditions on them.
