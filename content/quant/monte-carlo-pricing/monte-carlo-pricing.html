Monte Carlo Pricing

An option price is a discounted expected payoff under the risk-neutral measure. When no formula gives that expectation, simulate it: draw many paths, compute the payoff on each, and average. The law of large numbers makes it right, the central limit theorem tells you how right.

How to use this simulator

  1. Press Run. The estimate jumps around at first, then settles. The band narrows like $1/\sqrt N$.
  2. Turn on Antithetic. Each random draw $Z$ is reused as $-Z$, and the band gets visibly tighter for the same number of paths.
  3. Switch to Asian. The payoff uses the average price along the path. There is no Black-Scholes line any more, but the Monte Carlo band still tells you how precise the answer is.
  4. Change the strike and volatility. Out-of-the-money options have fewer paths that pay, so their relative error is larger.

1. Pricing as an expectation

Under the risk-neutral measure the stock drifts at the risk-free rate $r$. The price of a payoff $h$ is its discounted expectation, and Monte Carlo replaces the expectation with a sample average over $N$ simulated paths:

$$V_0 = e^{-rT}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb Q}\big[h(S)\big] \approx e^{-rT}\,\frac1N\sum_{k=1}^{N} h\big(S^{(k)}\big), \qquad S_{t+\Delta t} = S_t\, e^{(r - \frac12\sigma^2)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z}$$

The real-world drift $\mu$ is not used. Using it would give the expected payoff, not the price.

2. The error bar

By the central limit theorem the estimate is roughly normal around the true price with standard error $s/\sqrt N$, where $s$ is the sample standard deviation of the discounted payoffs:

$$\hat V \pm 1.96\,\frac{s}{\sqrt N}$$

The error falls slowly: ten times more precision costs a hundred times more paths. In exchange, the cost does not grow with the number of underlying assets, which is why Monte Carlo wins for baskets and path-dependent products where trees and grids explode.

3. Variance reduction

  • Antithetic variates: for every draw $Z$ also use $-Z$. The two payoffs are negatively correlated, so their average has lower variance.
  • Control variates: simulate something with a known price alongside, such as the European call or the geometric Asian, and correct the estimate by its observed error.
  • Importance sampling: draw more paths where the payoff is non-zero, then reweight. Useful for deep out-of-the-money options.
  • Quasi-random numbers: low-discrepancy sequences such as Sobol fill the space more evenly than random draws.
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
Z = rng.standard_normal((N, steps))
if antithetic: Z = np.vstack([Z, -Z])
dt = T / steps
logS = np.log(S0) + np.cumsum((r - 0.5*sigma**2)*dt + sigma*np.sqrt(dt)*Z, axis=1)
S = np.exp(logS)
pay = np.maximum(S.mean(axis=1) - K, 0)             # Asian call; use S[:, -1] for European
disc = np.exp(-r*T) * pay
price, se = disc.mean(), disc.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(disc))

Pitfalls

  • With antithetic pairs, compute the standard error from the pair averages, not from all $2N$ payoffs, which are not independent.
  • Time steps matter for path-dependent payoffs. A monitored average or barrier needs the same dates as the contract.
  • Fix the seed when comparing prices or computing Greeks by bumping inputs, or random noise swamps the difference.
  • Plain Monte Carlo cannot price American options, because exercise depends on the future. The Longstaff-Schwartz method fixes this with a regression.
