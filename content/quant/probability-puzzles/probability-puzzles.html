Probability puzzles

Quant interviews test whether you can turn a short story about dice, coins or sticks into an equation, fast and without fooling yourself. A handful of tools solve most of them: linearity of expectation, first-step analysis, symmetry and the complement. Then simulate to check.

1. The toolkit

  
Almost every interview puzzle falls to one of five moves. Name the move before you start computing.

Indicators are the most underused. For any event $A$, the indicator $\mathbf{1}_A$ is 1 when $A$ happens and 0 otherwise, so $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_A] = P(A)$. Counting things then becomes adding probabilities, with no need to know how the events depend on each other.

  
If the question says "expected number", look for indicators. If it says "expected time", look for states.






  
2. Waiting times

  
1. Rolls until the first six

  
Condition on the first roll. With probability $\tfrac16$ you are done in one roll. Otherwise one roll is spent and you are back where you started:

  
$$E = 1 + \tfrac56 E \quad\Rightarrow\quad E = 6$$

  
The same argument gives $1/p$ for any success probability $p$.


  
2. Rolls until two sixes in a row

  
Let $E$ be the expected remaining rolls from scratch, and $E_1$ just after a six. From scratch, a six moves you to state 1, anything else resets. From state 1, a six ends the game:

  
$$E = 1 + \tfrac16 E_1 + \tfrac56 E, \qquad E_1 = 1 + \tfrac56 E \quad\Rightarrow\quad E = 42$$

  
In general, $k$ successes in a row at chance $p$ take $\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{p^2} + \dots + \frac{1}{p^k}$ tries. For two sixes that is $6 + 36$.


  
3. HH versus HT

  
Flip a fair coin. Which takes longer on average to appear, HH or HT? They look symmetric, but they are not. For HT, once you see an H you only wait for a T, and extra H's keep you in place. For HH, a T after the first H sends you all the way back:

  
$$\mathbb{E}[\text{HT}] = 2 + 2 = 4, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\text{HH}] = 2 + 4 = 6$$

  
The difference is self-overlap. HH can overlap with itself shifted by one, HT cannot. A pattern that overlaps itself tends to arrive in clusters, so the waits between clusters are longer. The same logic, written as a Markov chain, handles any pattern.


  
4. Rolls to see every face

  
This is the coupon collector problem. Once you have seen $i$ faces, a new face appears with chance $\frac{6-i}{6}$, so that stage takes $\frac{6}{6-i}$ rolls on average. Add the stages:

  
$$\mathbb{E}[T] = \sum_{i=0}^{5} \frac{6}{6-i} = 6\left(1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac15 + \tfrac16\right) = 14.7$$

  
For $n$ coupons it is $n H_n \approx n \ln n + 0.577n$. The last few coupons dominate: the final face alone takes 6 rolls on average.






  
3. Decisions

  
5. Roll, with one optional reroll

  
You roll a die and are paid the face value in dollars. You may reroll once, but then you must take the second roll. When should you reroll, and what is the game worth? A second roll is worth $3.5$ on average, so keep any first roll above $3.5$:

  
$$V = \underbrace{\tfrac16(4 + 5 + 6)}_{\text{keep 4, 5, 6}} + \underbrace{\tfrac36 \cdot 3.5}_{\text{reroll 1, 2, 3}} = 2.5 + 1.75 = 4.25$$

  
This is backward induction, the core of optimal stopping and of pricing American options on a binomial tree: compare the value of stopping now with the expected value of continuing, and take the larger. With two rerolls, keep a first roll of 5 or 6 only, and the value rises to $4.67$.


  
How much to bet: the Kelly criterion

  
A coin lands heads with chance $p = 0.6$ and pays even money. Betting everything every time almost surely goes broke eventually. The fraction $f$ of wealth that maximizes long-run growth maximizes the expected log return:

  
$$g(f) = p \ln(1 + f) + q \ln(1 - f), \qquad g'(f) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; f^* = p - q = 0.2$$

  
For odds of $b$ to 1, $f^* = p - q/b$. Betting more than about $2f^*$ gives negative growth even though every bet has positive expected value. Many traders bet half-Kelly, giving up a quarter of the growth rate for half the volatility.






  
4. Random walks and coincidences

  
6. Gambler's ruin

  
You have \$3 and bet \$1 at a time on a fair coin, stopping at \$0 or \$10. Let $P_k$ be the chance of reaching 10 from $k$. First-step analysis gives $P_k = \tfrac12 P_{k+1} + \tfrac12 P_{k-1}$, so $P_k$ is a straight line from $P_0 = 0$ to $P_{10} = 1$:

  
$$P_k = \frac{k}{N} = 0.3, \qquad \text{and with win chance } p \ne \tfrac12: \quad P_k = \frac{1 - (q/p)^k}{1 - (q/p)^N}$$

  
A shortcut: a fair game is a martingale, so your expected final wealth equals your starting wealth, $10 \cdot P + 0 \cdot (1-P) = 3$. The expected game length is $k(N - k) = 21$ bets. A slightly unfair game is brutal: at $p = 0.49$ the chance of reaching \$10 from \$3 drops to about 0.26, and from \$30 to \$100 it falls from 0.3 to about 0.04. See also random walks and Brownian motion.


  
7. Shared birthdays

  
How many people until the chance that two share a birthday passes one half? Use the complement: everyone different.

  
$$P(\text{match}) = 1 - \prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(1 - \frac{i}{365}\right) \;\approx\; 1 - e^{-n(n-1)/730}, \qquad n = 23 \Rightarrow 0.507$$

  
The intuition is pairs: 23 people form $\binom{23}{2} = 253$ pairs, each matching with chance $\tfrac{1}{365}$. Collisions appear around $\sqrt{N}$ items for $N$ possible values, which is why hash collisions and ID clashes come sooner than expected.


  
8. A broken stick

  
Break a stick at two uniform random points. What is the chance the three pieces form a triangle? They do exactly when no piece is longer than half the stick. Each piece is longer than half with probability $\tfrac14$, and at most one piece can be, so by the complement:

  
$$P(\text{triangle}) = 1 - 3 \cdot \tfrac14 = \tfrac14$$






  
5. Where e shows up

  
9. Uniforms until the sum passes 1

  
Draw uniform numbers on $[0,1]$ until their sum exceeds 1. The chance that the first $n$ draws still sum to at most 1 is the volume of a simplex, $\tfrac{1}{n!}$. Since $\mathbb{E}[N] = \sum_{n \ge 0} P(N > n)$:

  
$$\mathbb{E}[N] = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n!} = e \approx 2.718$$


  
10. Hats and fixed points

  
$n$ people throw their hats in a pile and each takes one at random. The expected number who get their own hat is exactly 1, for any $n$: person $i$ gets theirs with chance $\tfrac1n$, and linearity adds $n$ of those. The chance that nobody does needs inclusion-exclusion:

  
$$P(\text{no fixed point}) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{e} \approx 0.368$$

  
The limit is reached fast. By $n = 10$ it agrees with $1/e$ to seven digits.

8. Common mistakes

  
    
    
  • Assuming symmetry that is not there, as with HH and HT.
    • 
    
  • Confusing $\mathbb{E}[1/X]$ with $1/\mathbb{E}[X]$. By Jensen's inequality they differ whenever $X$ varies. The same trap sinks naive averages of Sharpe ratios and of prices versus returns.
    • 
    
  • The gambler's fallacy. Independent flips have no memory. Waiting times do have structure, but it comes from the stopping rule, not the coin.
    • 
    
  • Conditioning on the wrong event. "At least one child is a boy" and "the older child is a boy" give different answers, $\tfrac13$ and $\tfrac12$ for two boys. See Bayes' theorem for Monty Hall and its cousins.
    • 
    
  • Maximizing expected value when the game repeats. Wealth compounds, so growth comes from the expected log, which is why Kelly bets a fraction.
    •
