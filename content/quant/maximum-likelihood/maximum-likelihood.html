Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Pick the parameters that make the data you actually saw as probable as possible. That one rule fits almost every model in quant finance, from a normal distribution to GARCH, and it also tells you how uncertain the fit is.

1. Likelihood

A model with parameters $\theta$ assigns a density $f(x \mid \theta)$ to each observation. For independent observations, the likelihood of $\theta$ is the product of those densities, read as a function of $\theta$ with the data held fixed:

$$L(\theta) = \prod_{t=1}^{n} f(x_t \mid \theta), \qquad \ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta) = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \log f(x_t \mid \theta)$$

The logarithm turns a product of tiny numbers into a sum, which is easier to compute and to differentiate, and it has the same maximum. The maximum likelihood estimate is $\hat\theta = \arg\max_\theta \ell(\theta)$.

2. The normal case in closed form

For a normal model the log-likelihood is

$$\ell(\mu,\sigma) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(x_t-\mu)^2$$

Setting both partial derivatives to zero gives the familiar answers. Note that the MLE of the variance divides by $n$, not $n-1$, so it is slightly biased in small samples.

$$\hat\mu = \bar x, \qquad \hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(x_t-\bar x)^2$$

Least squares regression is the same thing: if the errors are normal, maximizing likelihood is minimizing the sum of squared residuals. Logistic regression and GARCH have no closed form, so their MLE is found numerically, usually with Newton's method or a quasi-Newton optimizer on $-\ell$.

3. Curvature is uncertainty

A sharp peak means few parameter values fit the data well. The curvature at the peak is the Fisher information, and its inverse gives the variance of the estimate for large samples:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) \approx \left(-\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \theta^2}\Big|_{\hat\theta}\right)^{-1}, \qquad \text{for the normal mean: } \operatorname{SE}(\hat\mu) = \frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt n}$$

The green band in Fig 1 is $\hat\mu \pm 2\,\operatorname{SE}$. Comparing log-likelihoods of two nested models also gives the likelihood ratio test: twice the gain in $\ell$ is roughly chi-squared distributed, with one degree of freedom per extra parameter.

from scipy.optimize import minimize
import numpy as np
def nll(p, x):                               # negative log-likelihood
    mu, log_s = p; s = np.exp(log_s)            # optimize log sigma so sigma stays positive
    return 0.5 * np.sum(np.log(2*np.pi*s*s) + ((x - mu) / s)**2)

Pitfalls

  • MLE is only as good as the model. Fitting a normal to fat-tailed returns gives the right $\sigma$ but badly underestimates extreme days.
  • Optimizers find local maxima. For GARCH and mixtures, try several starting points.
  • Keep parameters in range, for example by optimizing $\log\sigma$ instead of $\sigma$.
  • Likelihood always rises with more parameters. Compare models with AIC, BIC or out-of-sample likelihood, not raw $\ell$.
