Cointegration and Pairs Trading

Two stock prices can each wander like a random walk and still be tied together, so that a fixed mix of them keeps coming back to the same level. That is cointegration. It is the statistical basis of pairs trading: buy the cheap leg, sell the rich one, and wait for the gap to close.

A pair, its spread and a trade

Two log prices are simulated. $X$ is a random walk and $Y = \beta X + s$, where the spread $s$ is an AR(1) with persistence $\phi$. The first half is the formation window: the hedge ratio, spread mean and spread volatility are estimated there only. The second half is traded out of sample.

How to use this simulator

  1. With $\phi = 0.95$ the true spread has a half-life of about 14 days. The test statistic is usually below its critical value, and the strategy tends to make money out of sample.
  2. Move $\phi$ to 1. The spread is now a random walk, so the pair is not cointegrated. The regression still finds a hedge ratio, but the test fails and the trades lose or drift.
  3. Raise the entry threshold. You trade less often, win more often per trade, and wait longer between trades.
  4. Add costs. A fast-reverting spread with many trades is hit hardest. Press "New data" to see how much the result moves between samples.

1. Integrated series

A series is integrated of order 1, written $I(1)$, if it is non-stationary but its first difference is stationary. Log prices are the usual example: the price wanders, the return does not. Regressing one $I(1)$ series on another is dangerous. Two independent random walks regularly give a high $R^2$ and a "significant" slope. This is the spurious regression problem.

2. Cointegration

Two $I(1)$ series $X_t$ and $Y_t$ are cointegrated if some combination of them is stationary:

$$s_t = Y_t - \beta X_t - c \;\sim\; I(0)$$

The vector $(1, -\beta)$ is the cointegrating vector and $\beta$ is the hedge ratio. Each price can drift anywhere, but they drift together, so the spread keeps returning to its mean. Economically this needs a reason: two share classes of one company, a stock and its ADR, two firms with the same costs and customers, or a future and its underlying.

3. The Engle-Granger test

The two-step test is the simplest way to check a pair:

  1. Regress $Y_t$ on $X_t$ by least squares to get $\hat\beta$, $\hat c$ and residuals $\hat s_t$.
  2. Run a Dickey-Fuller regression on the residuals and look at the t-statistic of $\gamma$:
$$\Delta\hat s_t = \gamma\,\hat s_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad H_0: \gamma = 0 \ \text{(unit root, no cointegration)}$$

Because $\hat\beta$ was chosen to make the residuals look as stationary as possible, the usual Dickey-Fuller critical values are too lenient. For two series with a constant, the 5% critical value is about $-3.34$ rather than $-2.86$. With more than two assets, the Johansen test finds several cointegrating vectors at once.

4. Trading the spread

Model the spread as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process and standardize it with the formation-window mean and standard deviation:

$$z_t = \frac{\hat s_t - \bar s}{\hat\sigma_s}, \qquad t_{1/2} = \frac{-\ln 2}{\ln \hat\phi}$$

A simple rule: when $z_t > k$ the spread is rich, so sell $Y$ and buy $\hat\beta$ units of $X$. When $z_t < -k$ do the opposite. Close when $z_t$ crosses 0. The position is roughly market neutral, so the profit comes from the spread converging, not from the market's direction.

import numpy as np
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import coint
form, trade = slice(0, n // 2), slice(n // 2, n)
t_stat, p_value, _ = coint(y[form], x[form])          # Engle-Granger
beta, c = np.polyfit(x[form], y[form], 1)
s = y - beta * x - c
z = (s - s[form].mean()) / s[form].std()               # formation stats only
phi = np.polyfit(s[form][:-1], s[form][1:], 1)[0]
half_life = -np.log(2) / np.log(phi)

Pitfalls

  • Searching thousands of pairs for the ones that pass the test is multiple testing. At a 5% level, about 1 in 20 unrelated pairs passes by chance, so demand an economic reason and an out-of-sample check.
  • Cointegration breaks. Mergers, index changes, new regulation or one firm's bad news can turn a stationary spread into a random walk. Use a stop loss on the z-score and on holding time, for example several half-lives.
  • Estimating $\beta$, the mean and the volatility on the full sample and then trading the same sample is look-ahead. Fit on past data only, as the simulator does, or on a rolling window.
  • The regression of $Y$ on $X$ and of $X$ on $Y$ give different hedge ratios. Total least squares or the Johansen method treat the two legs symmetrically.
  • Shorting has costs: borrow fees, recalls and the risk that the short leg is hard to borrow just when you need it.
