Expectation, Variance and Covariance

Expectation is the average outcome, variance is how far outcomes spread around it, and covariance is how two things move together. With these three numbers you can already measure the risk of a portfolio.

1. Expectation: the long-run average

The expectation of a random variable $X$ weights every outcome by its probability. For a die it is $3.5$, even though you never roll a $3.5$. For daily returns it is the drift you would earn per day on average.

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_i x_i\,p_i \qquad \text{or} \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \int x\,f(x)\,dx$$

Expectation is linear, with no conditions at all: $\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y]$. So the expected return of a portfolio is just the weighted average of the expected returns of its parts.

2. Variance: the spread

Variance is the expected squared distance from the mean. Its square root, the standard deviation $\sigma$, has the same unit as $X$. In finance $\sigma$ of returns is called volatility.

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2\big] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mathbb{E}[X]^2$$

Variance is not linear. Scaling multiplies it by the square, $\operatorname{Var}(aX) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$, and a sum picks up a cross term.

3. Covariance and correlation

Covariance measures whether two variables are above their means at the same time. Dividing by both standard deviations gives the correlation $\rho$, which always lies between $-1$ and $1$.

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)\big], \qquad \rho = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y}$$

The cross term is what makes diversification work. For a portfolio $P = wA + (1-w)B$:

$$\sigma_P^2 = w^2\sigma_A^2 + (1-w)^2\sigma_B^2 + 2w(1-w)\,\rho\,\sigma_A\sigma_B$$

When $\rho = 1$ the risk is a straight line between the two assets. Any $\rho < 1$ makes $\sigma_P$ smaller than that line, and with $\rho = -1$ you can reach zero risk. Setting the derivative to zero gives the minimum-risk weight:

$$w^* = \frac{\sigma_B^2 - \rho\,\sigma_A\sigma_B}{\sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2 - 2\rho\,\sigma_A\sigma_B}$$

With $n$ assets the same idea becomes a matrix. If $\Sigma$ is the covariance matrix and $w$ the weight vector, then $\sigma_P^2 = w^\top \Sigma\, w$.

4. Estimating from data

You never see the true values, only a sample. The usual estimators divide by $n-1$ so the variance estimate is unbiased:

$$\bar x = \frac1n\sum_{t=1}^n x_t, \qquad s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{t=1}^n (x_t-\bar x)^2$$

Means are much harder to estimate than volatilities. The error of $\bar x$ shrinks like $\sigma/\sqrt n$, and daily drifts are tiny compared with daily $\sigma$. Lower the number of days in Fig 1 and watch the sample mean jump around while the sample $\sigma$ stays close to the truth.

import numpy as np
R = np.column_stack([ret_a, ret_b])         # T x 2 daily returns
mu = R.mean(axis=0)                          # expectation per asset
S = np.cov(R, rowvar=False)               # 2 x 2 covariance, divides by n-1
w = np.array([0.4, 0.6])
port_mean, port_vol = w @ mu, np.sqrt(w @ S @ w)

Pitfalls

  • Correlation only sees linear co-movement. Two series can be strongly dependent and still have $\rho \approx 0$.
  • Correlations are not stable. In a crash many assets fall together and $\rho$ jumps toward 1, exactly when you need diversification.
  • Volatility scales with the square root of time: $\sigma_{\text{year}} \approx \sigma_{\text{day}}\sqrt{252}$, while the mean scales with time itself.
  • Heavy tails make sample variance unreliable. One extreme day can move it a lot.
