No-Arbitrage and Put-Call Parity

If two portfolios pay exactly the same in every future state, they must cost the same today. Otherwise you buy the cheap one, sell the expensive one, and pocket the gap with no risk. This single rule prices forwards and links calls to puts without any model.

Find the free lunch

Set the market prices of a European call and put with the same strike and expiry. If they break parity, the simulator shows which legs to buy and sell. The right chart is that trade's value at expiry for every possible stock price.

How to use this simulator

  1. Start with Fair prices. Both bars on the left are equal and there is nothing to trade.
  2. Raise the call price. The left side of the parity gets too expensive: the trade sells the call and buys the synthetic call made of a put, the stock and a loan.
  3. Look at the right chart. The profit is a flat line: it is the same whatever the stock does.
  4. Change the rate or the time to expiry and watch the present value of the strike move.

1. Replication

Compare two portfolios that you hold until the expiry date $T$:

  • A: one call with strike $K$, plus a zero-coupon bond that pays $K$ at $T$.
  • B: one put with strike $K$, plus one share of the stock.

If $S_T > K$, A is worth $(S_T-K)+K = S_T$ and B is worth $0 + S_T$. If $S_T \le K$, A is worth $0 + K$ and B is worth $(K-S_T)+S_T = K$. In every state both are worth $\max(S_T, K)$. So they must cost the same today:

$$C + K e^{-rT} = P + S_0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad C - P = S_0 - K e^{-rT}$$

No volatility, no probabilities and no model of the stock appear. That is why the relation holds so tightly in real markets, up to costs, dividends and borrowing limits.

2. The arbitrage

If $C - P > S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$, the call side is too expensive. Sell portfolio A and buy B: sell the call, buy the put, buy the stock, and borrow $Ke^{-rT}$. You receive the gap today, and at expiry the positions cancel exactly. If the inequality goes the other way, do the opposite.

An arbitrage is a trade that costs nothing, can never lose, and sometimes gains. In a working market such gaps close within moments.

3. The forward price

The same argument prices a forward. Buying the stock today with borrowed money and holding it gives exactly the stock at $T$, at a cost of $S_0 e^{rT}$ paid then. A forward contract gives the same thing for $F_0$. So

$$F_0 = S_0\,e^{rT}, \qquad \text{and with a dividend yield } q:\; F_0 = S_0\,e^{(r-q)T}$$

Put-call parity is a forward in disguise: a long call and a short put with the same strike pay $S_T - K$, which is a forward at price $K$.

Pitfalls

  • Parity holds for European options. American puts can be worth more than parity says, because early exercise has value.
  • Dividends paid before expiry lower the forward. Replace $S_0$ with $S_0$ minus the present value of the dividends.
  • Real arbitrage needs bid-ask spreads, fees, short-sale costs and funding at the rate you can actually borrow. Many apparent violations vanish once you add them.
  • Small mispricings with large size still carry execution and settlement risk. "Riskless" is a model statement.
