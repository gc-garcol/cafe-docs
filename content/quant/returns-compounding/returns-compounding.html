Returns, Compounding and Time Value

Money today is worth more than the same money later, because it can earn interest in between. This page covers the three tools that follow from that: compounding, discounting, and the difference between simple and log returns.

Grow and shrink a dollar

Compounding mode grows $1 at a fixed rate and compares how often interest is paid. Volatility drag mode adds random yearly returns and shows why the typical investor ends up with less than the average return promises.

How to use this simulator

  1. In Compounding, raise the rate to 20% and compare the curves. Paying more often helps, but the gain stops at the continuous limit $e^{rT}$.
  2. Move the cash flow year marker. The readout shows what that future $100 is worth today.
  3. Switch to Volatility drag. Keep the mean return fixed and raise the volatility. The average path keeps its slope, but the median path bends down.
  4. Set volatility to 50% and mean to 0%: the median investor loses money even though the average yearly return is zero.
Numbers

1. Simple and compound growth

At rate $r$ per year paid $m$ times a year, $1 grows to

$$V_T = \left(1+\frac{r}{m}\right)^{mT} \;\xrightarrow{\;m\to\infty\;}\; e^{rT}$$

The limit is continuous compounding. It is what most pricing models use, because exponentials are easy to multiply, differentiate and invert. A quoted rate only means something together with its compounding convention: 8% compounded monthly is an effective yearly rate of $(1+0.08/12)^{12}-1 = 8.30\%$.

2. Discounting: the time value of money

Run compounding backwards and you get the present value of a future cash flow $C$. A stream of cash flows is worth the sum of their present values:

$$PV = C\,e^{-rT}, \qquad PV = \sum_{i} C_i\,e^{-r t_i}$$

This one formula prices bonds, loans and the "$K e^{-rT}$" strike term in option pricing. Higher rates and later dates both make future money worth less today.

3. Simple returns and log returns

For a price going from $P_{t-1}$ to $P_t$ there are two common definitions:

$$R_t = \frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}} - 1, \qquad r_t = \ln\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}} = \ln(1+R_t)$$

For small moves they are almost equal, because $\ln(1+R) \approx R - R^2/2$. That small $-R^2/2$ term is where the trouble starts.

4. Volatility drag

A stock that gains 50% and then loses 50% has an average simple return of 0%, but ends at $1.5 \times 0.5 = 0.75$. Compounding punishes volatility. For returns with arithmetic mean $\mu$ and volatility $\sigma$, the growth rate of a typical path is roughly

$$g \approx \mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}$$

The mean of final wealth still grows at $\mu$, but it is pulled up by a few lucky paths. The median path grows at $g$. The same $-\sigma^2/2$ shows up in geometric Brownian motion and in Itô's lemma.

R = prices.pct_change()                    # simple returns
r = np.log(prices).diff()                  # log returns, these add over time
total_simple = (1 + R).prod() - 1
total_log = r.sum()                        # equals log(1 + total_simple)
cagr = np.exp(r.mean() * 252) - 1          # growth rate, includes the drag

Pitfalls

  • Never average simple returns over time to get a growth rate. Use the geometric mean or the mean log return.
  • Never add log returns across assets. Portfolio returns are weighted sums of simple returns.
  • Leverage multiplies $\sigma^2$ by the square of the leverage, so drag grows fast. Daily-rebalanced leveraged funds decay in sideways markets for this reason.
  • Annualize consistently: daily log returns times 252, daily volatility times $\sqrt{252}$.
