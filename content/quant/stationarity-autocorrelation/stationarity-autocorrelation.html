Stationarity and Autocorrelation

Most statistics assume the thing you measure keeps the same behaviour over time. A series with that property is stationary. Returns are close to it, prices are not, and the autocorrelation function is the first tool to tell them apart.

One dial from noise to random walk

The series follows $x_t = \phi\,x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$ with standard normal shocks. The bottom chart is its sample autocorrelation: how much $x_t$ resembles $x_{t-k}$ for each lag $k$.

How to use this simulator

  1. Set $\phi = 0$. The series is white noise and every bar stays inside the grey band, which is what chance alone produces.
  2. Set $\phi = 0.8$. The series wanders but keeps coming back to 0. The bars decay like $0.8^k$, and the readout gives the half-life of a shock.
  3. Set $\phi = 1$. Now it is a random walk: no pull back, the two halves of the sample have different means, and the bars barely decay.
  4. Turn on Difference. The random walk becomes white noise again. That is why models use returns rather than prices.

1. Stationarity

A series is weakly stationary if its mean and variance do not depend on time, and the covariance between $x_t$ and $x_{t-k}$ depends only on the lag $k$:

$$\mathbb E[x_t] = \mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(x_t) = \sigma^2, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(x_t, x_{t-k}) = \gamma_k \quad \text{for all } t$$

Only then does "the average of the sample" estimate one fixed number. For a non-stationary series, a longer sample does not give a better estimate, it gives a different one.

2. Autocorrelation

The autocorrelation at lag $k$ is $\rho_k = \gamma_k / \gamma_0$, estimated by

$$\hat\rho_k = \frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^{n}(x_t-\bar x)(x_{t-k}-\bar x)}{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(x_t-\bar x)^2}$$

For white noise every $\hat\rho_k$ is roughly $\mathcal N(0, 1/n)$, so bars outside $\pm 1.96/\sqrt n$ are evidence of dependence. With 20 lags, about one will cross by chance. The Ljung-Box test checks many lags at once.

3. AR(1), mean reversion and unit roots

For $|\phi| < 1$ the AR(1) process is stationary with $\rho_k = \phi^k$ and variance $1/(1-\phi^2)$. A shock fades by half after

$$h = \frac{\ln 0.5}{\ln \phi}$$

steps. That half-life is the number pairs traders look at when they model a spread as mean-reverting. At $\phi = 1$ the process has a unit root: shocks never fade, the variance grows like $t$, and the series is a random walk. The augmented Dickey-Fuller test asks whether $\phi = 1$ can be rejected.

from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import acf, adfuller
r = np.log(prices).diff().dropna()          # returns: close to stationary
print(acf(r, nlags=20))
stat, pvalue, *_ = adfuller(np.log(prices))  # unit root test on log prices
# a large p-value means a unit root cannot be rejected: difference before modelling

Pitfalls

  • Regressing one random walk on another gives high $R^2$ and big t-statistics with no real relation: a spurious regression. Difference first, or test for cointegration.
  • With $\phi$ near 1, a sample of a few hundred points looks almost the same as a random walk. Unit-root tests have low power there.
  • Returns have little autocorrelation, but squared returns have a lot. No autocorrelation does not mean independence, see GARCH.
  • Structural breaks such as a regime change look like non-stationarity. Check sub-periods before trusting a full-sample estimate.
