Value at Risk and Drawdown

Volatility says how much returns wiggle. Risk managers want something sharper: how bad a bad day is, how bad the days beyond that are, and how deep a losing streak can get. VaR, expected shortfall and drawdown answer those three questions.

How to use this simulator

  1. Keep Normal returns. Historical VaR and normal VaR agree, and breaches happen about as often as the confidence level says.
  2. Switch to Fat tails with 3 degrees of freedom. The volatility is the same, but more days are quiet and a few are extreme. At 95% the two VaRs are close, yet expected shortfall moves further out.
  3. Raise the confidence to 99% and the days to 2500. Now the normal VaR sits well inside the real tail and is breached more often than promised. Fat tails hurt most far out.
  4. Press New sample a few times and watch the maximum drawdown jump around. It is a very noisy statistic.

1. Value at Risk

The one-day VaR at confidence $c$ is the loss that is exceeded only with probability $1-c$. It is a quantile of the return distribution, with the sign flipped so that it reads as a loss:

$$\text{VaR}_c = -\,q_{1-c}(R), \qquad \text{normal model: } \text{VaR}_c = -\big(\mu + z_{1-c}\,\sigma\big)$$

"95% one-day VaR of $1m" means: on 19 days out of 20, the loss should be smaller than $1m. Historical VaR reads the quantile from past returns. Parametric VaR assumes a distribution, usually normal, and uses its quantile, $z_{0.05} = -1.645$. Horizons are often scaled with $\sqrt{h}$, which assumes independent days.

2. Expected shortfall

VaR says where the tail starts, not how long it is. Expected shortfall, also called CVaR, is the average loss on the days beyond VaR:

$$\text{ES}_c = -\,\mathbb E\big[R \,\big|\, R \le -\text{VaR}_c\big]$$

ES is always at least VaR, and the gap is large for fat tails. It is also subadditive: the ES of a combined portfolio is never above the sum of the parts, which VaR does not guarantee. Bank regulation moved from 99% VaR to 97.5% ES for this reason.

3. Drawdown

Drawdown measures the pain of holding a strategy over time: the drop from the highest value so far.

$$\text{DD}_t = 1 - \frac{V_t}{\max_{s \le t} V_s}, \qquad \text{MDD} = \max_t \text{DD}_t$$

Investors leave during long drawdowns, and leverage can force you to sell at the bottom, so maximum drawdown and the time to recover matter as much as volatility. The Calmar ratio divides yearly return by maximum drawdown.

VaR_hist = -np.quantile(returns, 1 - c)
ES_hist = -returns[returns <= -VaR_hist].mean()
equity = (1 + returns).cumprod()
drawdown = 1 - equity / equity.cummax()
max_dd = drawdown.max()
breaches = (returns < -VaR_model).mean()       # should be close to 1 - c

Pitfalls

  • VaR says nothing about losses beyond it. Two portfolios with the same VaR can have very different worst days.
  • Historical VaR only knows the past window. A calm two-year sample hides crisis risk.
  • Backtest a VaR model by counting breaches. Too many, or breaches that come in clusters, mean the model is wrong. The Kupiec test formalizes the count.
  • Maximum drawdown grows with the length of the sample. Compare strategies only over the same period.
