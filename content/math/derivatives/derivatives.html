Derivatives

The derivative is the instant rate of change: the slope of a curve at a single point. It is what you get when a secant line is squeezed into a tangent, and it turns one function into another that records the slope everywhere.

1. Average vs instant rate

A ball dropped from rest falls $s(t) = 5t^2$ meters after $t$ seconds. Between $t = 1$ and $t = 1 + h$ its average speed is distance over time:

$$\frac{s(1+h) - s(1)}{h} = \frac{5(1 + 2h + h^2) - 5}{h} = 10 + 5h$$
Gap h (s)Average speed (m/s)

The averages close in on $10$ m/s. That is the speed at the instant $t = 1$, the number a speedometer would show. Geometrically, each average is the slope of a secant line on the graph of $s$, and the instant speed is the slope of the tangent.

2. The definition

The slope between $(a, f(a))$ and $(a + h, f(a + h))$ is the difference quotient. The derivative is its limit as $h \to 0$. If this limit does not exist, $f$ is not differentiable at $a$.

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

The tangent line at $a$ is then the line through $(a, f(a))$ with that slope: $y = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$.

Two worked examples

For $f(x) = x^2$:

$$\frac{(a+h)^2 - a^2}{h} = \frac{2ah + h^2}{h} = 2a + h \;\longrightarrow\; 2a$$

So the slope of $x^2$ at any point is $2a$. At $a = 1$ it is exactly $2$, which you can check in the lab. For $f(x) = \frac{1}{x}$, combine the fractions first:

$$\frac{\frac{1}{a+h} - \frac{1}{a}}{h} = \frac{a - (a+h)}{h\,a(a+h)} = \frac{-1}{a(a+h)} \;\longrightarrow\; -\frac{1}{a^2}$$

Both follow the same pattern as the limits guide: the raw quotient is $\frac{0}{0}$ at $h = 0$, and algebra cancels the $h$.

Notation

The same derivative has several names. $f'(x)$ is Lagrange's notation. $\frac{dy}{dx}$ or $\frac{d}{dx} f(x)$ is Leibniz's, which reads as "a tiny change in $y$ over a tiny change in $x$" and makes the chain rule look like fraction cancelling. Physics often writes $\dot{x}$ for a derivative in time.

3. The derivative is a function

Computing $f'(a)$ at every $a$ gives a new function $f'$. The lower plot in Fig 1 draws it. Use "Sweep a" on $x^3 - 3x$ and watch three facts line up:

  • Where $f$ climbs, $f'$ is above the axis. Where $f$ falls, $f'$ is below.
  • At the peak $x = -1$ and the valley $x = 1$ the tangent is flat, so $f'$ crosses zero.
  • Where $f$ bends from hill to bowl ($x = 0$), $f'$ reaches its lowest point.

Try $\sin x$: its derivative curve is $\cos x$, the same wave shifted left by a quarter period. Try $e^x$: the derivative curve sits exactly on top of the function, because $e^x$ is its own derivative.

4. Where a derivative fails

Fig 2 · Differentiable means smooth: no corner, no break, no vertical tangent.

Pick $|x|$ in the lab and set $a$ to $0$. With the forward quotient the secant slope is $+1$ for every $h \gt 0$, but from the left it would be $-1$. The one-sided limits of the difference quotient disagree, so $f'(0)$ does not exist. The central quotient gives $0$, which is a trap: it is the average of two different slopes, not a derivative.

Differentiable implies continuous, but not the other way around. $|x|$ is continuous everywhere and fails to be differentiable at one point. There are even functions, like the Weierstrass function, that are continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere.

5. Rules you actually use

Nobody computes the limit each time. These rules, each proved once from the definition, cover nearly every function built from the usual pieces.

RuleDerivative
Constant$(c)' = 0$
Power$(x^n)' = n x^{n-1}$, for any real $n$
Sum, constant multiple$(c f + g)' = c f' + g'$
Product$(f g)' = f' g + f g'$
Quotient$\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f' g - f g'}{g^2}$
Chain$(f(g(x)))' = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$
Exponential, log$(e^x)' = e^x$, $(a^x)' = a^x \ln a$, $(\ln x)' = \frac{1}{x}$
Trig$(\sin x)' = \cos x$, $(\cos x)' = -\sin x$, $(\tan x)' = \frac{1}{\cos^2 x}$
Inverse trig$(\arctan x)' = \frac{1}{1 + x^2}$, $(\arcsin x)' = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}$

Worked examples

  1. Product: $\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^2 \sin x\right) = 2x \sin x + x^2 \cos x$.
  2. Quotient: $\frac{d}{dx} \frac{x}{x^2 + 1} = \frac{(x^2 + 1) - x \cdot 2x}{(x^2 + 1)^2} = \frac{1 - x^2}{(x^2 + 1)^2}$.
  3. Chain: $\frac{d}{dx} \sin(x^2) = \cos(x^2) \cdot 2x$. Outer derivative, evaluated at the inner function, times the inner derivative.
  4. Chain, twice: $\frac{d}{dx} e^{\sin 3x} = e^{\sin 3x} \cdot \cos 3x \cdot 3$.
  5. Root as a power: $\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{x} = \frac{d}{dx} x^{1/2} = \frac{1}{2} x^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$.

6. Why the rules are true

Power rule. Expand with the binomial theorem: $(x + h)^n = x^n + n x^{n-1} h + (\text{terms with } h^2 \text{ or higher})$. Subtract $x^n$, divide by $h$, and every term except $n x^{n-1}$ still carries an $h$, so it vanishes in the limit.

Product rule. Picture $f g$ as the area of a rectangle with sides $f$ and $g$. Nudge $x$: the sides grow by $\Delta f$ and $\Delta g$, and the area grows by $f\,\Delta g + g\,\Delta f + \Delta f\,\Delta g$. The last corner piece is a product of two small changes, so it shrinks faster than $h$ and drops out.

Chain rule. Rates multiply. If $u = g(x)$ changes 3 times as fast as $x$, and $y = f(u)$ changes 2 times as fast as $u$, then $y$ changes $6$ times as fast as $x$. In Leibniz notation:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}$$

The chain rule is also the engine of backpropagation in neural networks: the gradient of the loss is a long product of local derivatives, one per layer.

7. Linear approximation

Zoom in far enough on a differentiable curve and it looks like its tangent line. That gives a cheap estimate near any point where you know $f$ and $f'$:

$$f(a + \Delta x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)\,\Delta x$$

Example: estimate $\sqrt{4.1}$. With $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$, $a = 4$, $f(4) = 2$ and $f'(4) = \frac{1}{4}$, so $\sqrt{4.1} \approx 2 + \frac{0.1}{4} = 2.025$. The true value is $2.02485\ldots$. The error is about $\frac{1}{2} f''(a) \Delta x^2$, which the Taylor series guide makes precise.

Newton's method

Run linear approximation backwards to solve $f(x) = 0$. Replace $f$ by its tangent at the current guess, and jump to where the tangent hits zero:

$$x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$$

For $f(x) = x^2 - 2$ starting at $x_0 = 3$, the guesses are $1.8333$, $1.4621$, $1.41500$, $1.4142138$, $1.41421356237$. The number of correct digits roughly doubles each step.

8. Higher derivatives and shape

The derivative of $f'$ is the second derivative $f''$. It measures how the slope itself changes, which is how the curve bends.

f′′ > 0
bowl

Concave up. The slope is increasing, and tangent lines sit below the curve.

f′′ < 0
hill

Concave down. The slope is decreasing, and tangent lines sit above the curve.

f′′ changes sign
inflection

The bend flips. For $x^3 - 3x$ that happens at $x = 0$, where $f''(x) = 6x$ changes sign.

In motion, position $s(t)$ has velocity $s'(t)$ and acceleration $s''(t)$. For the falling ball, $s = 5t^2$, $s' = 10t$ and $s'' = 10$: constant acceleration, the familiar $g \approx 10\ \text{m/s}^2$.

9. Optimization

At a smooth peak or valley the tangent is flat. So to find extremes, solve $f'(x) = 0$ and test the candidates, along with the ends of the allowed interval.

f′ > 0
rising

The function increases around that point.

f′ < 0
falling

The function decreases around that point.

f′ = 0
flat

A peak, a valley, or a flat bend. If $f'' \lt 0$ it is a peak, if $f'' \gt 0$ a valley.

A worked problem

You have $100$ m of fence for a rectangular pen. Which shape has the largest area?

  1. Sides $x$ and $y$ with $2x + 2y = 100$, so $y = 50 - x$ and the area is $A(x) = x(50 - x) = 50x - x^2$.
  2. $A'(x) = 50 - 2x = 0$ gives $x = 25$.
  3. $A''(x) = -2 \lt 0$, so this is a maximum. The ends $x = 0$ and $x = 50$ give zero area.
  4. The best pen is a $25 \times 25$ square with area $625\ \text{m}^2$.

With many variables, the derivative becomes a gradient vector $\nabla f$ that points uphill. Gradient descent walks against it in small steps: $w \leftarrow w - \eta\,\nabla f(w)$. That single update trains almost every machine learning model.

10. The same thing in code

When you only have $f$ as a black box, approximate the limit with a small $h$. The central difference is the better default: its error shrinks like $h^2$ instead of $h$.

const forward = (f, x, h) => (f(x + h) - f(x)) / h;
const central = (f, x, h) => (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2 * h);

// exact: (sin)'(1) = cos(1) = 0.5403023058681398
forward(Math.sin, 1, 1e-1);   // 0.49736  error 4.3e-2
forward(Math.sin, 1, 1e-4);   // 0.54026  error 4.2e-5
central(Math.sin, 1, 1e-4);   // 0.540302305  error 9.0e-10
forward(Math.sin, 1, 1e-12);  // 0.54035  error 4.3e-5  (too small: rounding wins)

// Gradient descent on f(w) = (w - 3)^2, whose derivative is 2(w - 3)
let w = 5;
for (let i = 0; i < 50; i++) w -= 0.1 * 2 * (w - 3);
w;  // 3.0000285

There is a sweet spot for $h$. Too large and the secant is a poor tangent (truncation error). Too small and $f(x+h) - f(x)$ subtracts two nearly equal floats (rounding error). For forward differences in double precision the best $h$ is near $10^{-8}$. Libraries that need exact derivatives use automatic differentiation instead, which applies the chain rule to the program itself.

11. Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the inner derivative: $\frac{d}{dx}\sin(3x)$ is $3\cos(3x)$, not $\cos(3x)$.
  • Treating the product rule as $(fg)' = f'g'$. Test with $f = g = x$: $(x^2)' = 2x$, not $1$.
  • Applying the power rule to $e^x$ or $2^x$. The variable is in the exponent, so the exponential rule applies.
  • Assuming $f'(a) = 0$ means a peak or a valley. $x^3$ has a flat tangent at $0$ and keeps rising.
  • Plugging in the point before differentiating. The derivative of the number $f(2)$ is $0$; you want $f'(2)$.

12. Next

Derivatives take a function to its slope. The integral goes the other way, from a rate back to the accumulated total. The two are linked by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, covered in the Integrals guide. Stacking derivatives of every order at one point gives the Taylor series.
