Laplace Transform

The Laplace transform turns a function of time into a function of a new variable $s$. Calculus becomes algebra, which makes differential equations far easier to solve.

Try it first

Pick a signal $f(t)$. The transform multiplies it by a fading weight $e^{-st}$ and measures the area under the product. Slide $s$ and watch that area change. It always matches the closed-form answer $F(s)$.

$f(t)\,e^{-st}$ f(t) $e^{-st}$

1. The definition

For a function $f(t)$ defined for $t \ge 0$, the Laplace transform is an integral from zero to infinity.

$$F(s) = \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} = \int_0^\infty f(t)\, e^{-st}\,dt$$

The factor $e^{-st}$ shrinks the far future toward zero, so many signals that grow over time still give a finite area. Larger $s$ means a faster fade. The integral only exists when $s$ is large enough, and that range is called the region of convergence.

It is an integral, so everything from the integrals guide still applies.

2. A worked example

Take $f(t) = e^{-kt}$. Combine the exponents and integrate.

$$\int_0^\infty e^{-(s+k)t}\,dt = \frac{1}{s+k}$$

The upper limit vanishes because $s + k \gt 0$, and the lower limit leaves $\frac{1}{s+k}$. Choose $e^{-kt}$ in the lab with $s = 1$ and $k = 1$. The area should read $0.5$.

4. Why it helps: derivatives become multiplication

Integrating by parts once shows how the transform treats a derivative.

$$\mathcal{L}\{f'(t)\} = s F(s) - f(0)$$

Differentiating in time turns into multiplying by $s$, with the starting value folded in. A differential equation in $t$ becomes an algebraic equation in $s$. Solve it, then transform back.

5. Solving one by hand

Solve $y' + 2y = 0$ with $y(0) = 3$. Transform both sides.

$$sY - 3 + 2Y = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; Y(s) = \frac{3}{s+2}$$

From the table, $\frac{1}{s+k}$ is the transform of $e^{-kt}$. With $k = 2$ the answer is $y(t) = 3e^{-2t}$. No guessing a trial solution was needed.

6. Properties

Linear
split

$\mathcal{L}\{a f + b g\} = a F + b G$

Shift in s
damp

$\mathcal{L}\{e^{-at} f(t)\} = F(s+a)$

Delay in t
wait

$\mathcal{L}\{f(t-c)\,u(t-c)\} = e^{-cs} F(s)$

Another useful rule is the convolution theorem. A convolution in time, which is how a system responds to an input, becomes a plain product $F(s)\,G(s)$ in the s-domain. This is why engineers describe circuits, filters and control systems by their transfer functions.

7. Where it shows up

Electrical circuits with resistors, capacitors and inductors. Mechanical springs and dampers. Control systems, where poles of $F(s)$ reveal stability. Anywhere a linear system with a starting state responds to an input over time.
