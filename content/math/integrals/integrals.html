Integrals

The integral adds up infinitely many tiny pieces. Its most familiar job is the area under a curve, built from thin rectangles.

Try it first

The shaded rectangles estimate the area under the curve. Add more of them and the estimate closes in on the exact value. Switch between left, right and midpoint rules to see how each one errs.

rectangles curve

1. Area by slicing

The area under a rectangle-shaped graph is easy: width times height. For a curve, cut the interval [a, b] into n strips of width Δx = (b − a) / n. Pretend the curve is flat across each strip and add up the rectangles.

Sn = Σ f(xi) · Δx

The sample point xi can be the left edge, the right edge or the middle of each strip. For a rising curve the left rule underestimates and the right rule overestimates. The midpoint rule usually does better because the errors partly cancel.

2. The definition

Let the strips get thinner without limit. If the sums settle on one number, that number is the definite integral.

∫ab f(x) dx = limn→∞ Σ f(xi) Δx

The symbol ∫ is a stretched S for "sum". dx is what Δx becomes: a width too small to measure. Where f dips below the axis the area counts as negative, so the integral is a signed area.

An integral is a limit of sums, the same limit idea from the first guide.

3. The Fundamental Theorem

Computing a limit of sums is slow. The shortcut is to find an antiderivative F, a function with F′ = f. Then the total accumulated change is the difference of F at the ends.

∫ab f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)

Example with f(x) = x² on [0, 3]. An antiderivative is F(x) = x³/3, so the area is 27/3 − 0 = 9. Choose x² in the lab above and watch the sums head toward 9.

One technique worth knowing early is substitution, the chain rule in reverse. For ∫ 2x·cos(x²) dx, let u = x², so du = 2x dx. The integral becomes ∫ cos u du = sin(x²) + C.

6. Beyond area

Integrals accumulate any rate. Add up speed over time to get distance. Add up density over a length to get mass. Add up probability density over a range to get a probability. Whenever you see "total from a rate", think integral.
