Jacobian Determinant

A curved map bends the grid, so it has no single determinant. Zoom in far enough, though, and every smooth map looks linear. The determinant of that local linear map, the Jacobian determinant, tells how much the map stretches area at each point, and whether it flips it. It is the factor behind $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$ and every change of variables in an integral.

1. Start with one variable

For $y = f(x)$, a tiny interval of length $dx$ around $x$ lands on an interval of length about $|f'(x)|\,dx$. The derivative is the local stretch factor for length. Its sign says whether the interval keeps its direction or is reversed.

That is why substitution in a one-variable integral carries a derivative: with $x = g(u)$, $\int f(x)\,dx = \int f(g(u))\,g'(u)\,du$. The Jacobian determinant is the same idea for maps from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^n$: a stretch factor for area, volume, and their higher-dimensional versions.

2. The Jacobian matrix

Take a map $\mathbf{f}(u, v) = (x(u, v),\, y(u, v))$. Near a point $\mathbf{p}$, a small step $\mathbf{h}$ in the domain moves the output by approximately a linear function of $\mathbf{h}$:

$$\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{h}) \approx \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{p}) + J(\mathbf{p})\,\mathbf{h}, \qquad J = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u} & \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v} \\[2mm] \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v} \end{bmatrix}$$

Row $i$ holds the partial derivatives of output $i$; column $j$ is how the output moves when you nudge input $j$. In the lab, the blue edge of the image patch is close to the first column times $h$ and the red edge to the second column times $h$. For a map from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$ the matrix is $m \times n$, but only a square one has a determinant.

$J(\mathbf{p})$ is the best linear approximation of $\mathbf{f}$ at $\mathbf{p}$: the multivariable derivative, the same role the differential $dy = f'(x)\,dx$ plays in one variable.

3. The Jacobian determinant

A linear map scales every area by $|\det|$ of its matrix (see Determinants). Near $\mathbf{p}$, $\mathbf{f}$ behaves like the linear map $J(\mathbf{p})$, so small regions near $\mathbf{p}$ are scaled by $|\det J(\mathbf{p})|$:

$$\det J = \frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(u, v)} = \frac{\partial x}{\partial u}\frac{\partial y}{\partial v} - \frac{\partial x}{\partial v}\frac{\partial y}{\partial u}, \qquad \text{Area}\big(\mathbf{f}(\text{small region})\big) \approx |\det J|\cdot \text{Area}(\text{region})$$

Unlike a matrix determinant, this one is a function: it can change from point to point. The approximation becomes exact in the limit, which is what the ratio in the lab shows as $h \to 0$.

Sign of det JMeaning near the point
$\det J > 0$orientation kept: a counter-clockwise loop stays counter-clockwise
$\det J < 0$orientation flipped: the patch is mirrored
$\det J = 0$area is crushed to zero there; the map may fold, and has no smooth local inverse
The Jacobian determinant is the determinant of the map's best local linear approximation: a local, signed area-scaling factor.

4. Polar coordinates: where the r comes from

The map from $(r, \theta)$ to $(x, y)$ is $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$. Its Jacobian:

$$J = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det J = r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r$$

A small $dr \times d\theta$ box becomes a piece of a ring: $dr$ thick and $r\,d\theta$ long, because an arc of angle $d\theta$ at radius $r$ has length $r\,d\theta$. Far from the origin the same $d\theta$ sweeps a longer arc, so the patch is bigger. At $r = 0$ the whole side $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$ collapses to one point, and $\det J = 0$.

So $dA = dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta$. In fact the ring piece between radii $r_1$ and $r_2$ has area exactly $\tfrac{1}{2}(r_2^2 - r_1^2)\,\Delta\theta = \bar r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta$ with $\bar r$ the midpoint radius. The classic payoff is the Gaussian integral, impossible with ordinary antiderivatives but two lines in polar form:

$$\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx\right)^{2} = \iint_{\mathbb{R}^2} e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dx\,dy = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac{1}{2} = \pi$$

Hence $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx = \sqrt{\pi}$, the constant in the Gaussian distribution. The factor $r$ is what makes the inner integral solvable: $\int_0^\infty r e^{-r^2}\,dr = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

5. The change of variables formula

Chop the region $S$ in the $(u, v)$ plane into tiny squares. Each one maps to a tiny patch of $R = \mathbf{f}(S)$ with area $|\det J|\,du\,dv$. Summing $g$ times those areas gives:

$$\iint_{R} g(x, y)\,dx\,dy = \iint_{S} g\big(\mathbf{f}(u, v)\big)\,\left|\det J(u, v)\right|\,du\,dv$$

This holds when $\mathbf{f}$ is one-to-one on $S$ (apart from a set of zero area, like the line $r = 0$) and has continuous partial derivatives. The absolute value is there because areas in an integral are positive; the sign only tracks orientation.

Worked example: a tilted region

Compute $\iint_R (x + y)^2\,dA$ over the parallelogram $0 \le x + y \le 2$, $0 \le x - y \le 1$. The region is a rectangle in $u = x + y$, $v = x - y$. Invert: $x = \frac{u + v}{2}$, $y = \frac{u - v}{2}$.

  1. Jacobian of $(u, v) \mapsto (x, y)$: $\det\begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & -1/2 \end{bmatrix} = -\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{4} = -\frac{1}{2}$, so $|\det J| = \frac{1}{2}$.
  2. Rewrite: $\iint_R (x+y)^2\,dA = \int_0^1\!\int_0^2 u^2 \cdot \frac{1}{2}\,du\,dv$.
  3. Inner integral: $\int_0^2 u^2\,du = \frac{8}{3}$. Outer: $\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{8}{3}\cdot 1 = \frac{4}{3}$.
  4. Sanity check with $g = 1$: area of $R$ is $\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 1$. Its sides are $(1, 1)$ and $(\tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2})$, and $|1\cdot(-\tfrac{1}{2}) - 1\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}| = 1$. ✓

Note which direction the Jacobian goes: it is $\partial(x, y)/\partial(u, v)$, the derivative of the old variables with respect to the new ones. If you only have $u, v$ as functions of $x, y$, use the inverse rule $\frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(u, v)} = 1 \big/ \frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)}$. Here $\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)} = \det\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = -2$, and indeed $1/(-2) = -\frac{1}{2}$.

6. Three dimensions

In space the Jacobian is $3 \times 3$ and its determinant scales volume. The two standard coordinate systems:

CoordinatesMap|det J|Volume element
Cylindrical$x = r\cos\theta,\ y = r\sin\theta,\ z = z$$r$$dV = r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz$
Spherical$x = \rho\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\ y = \rho\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\ z = \rho\cos\varphi$$\rho^2\sin\varphi$$dV = \rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta$

A spherical box has edges $d\rho$, $\rho\,d\varphi$ and $\rho\sin\varphi\,d\theta$ (the circle of latitude has radius $\rho\sin\varphi$), which multiply to the same factor. Volume of a ball of radius $R$:

$$\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi}\!\!\int_0^{R} \rho^2\sin\varphi\;d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta = 2\pi \cdot 2 \cdot \frac{R^3}{3} = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$$

Here $\varphi$ is the angle from the $z$-axis. With the angle from the $xy$-plane instead, the factor becomes $\rho^2\cos$ of that angle, so check the convention before copying a formula.

7. When the determinant is zero

Inverse function theorem. If $\mathbf{f}$ has continuous partial derivatives and $\det J(\mathbf{p}) \ne 0$, then near $\mathbf{p}$ the map has a smooth inverse, and the inverse's Jacobian is the matrix inverse: $J_{\mathbf{f}^{-1}}(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{p})) = J_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{p})^{-1}$. Its determinant is therefore $1/\det J_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{p})$.

Where $\det J = 0$ this can fail. In the lab:

  • Fold, $(u, v) \mapsto (u,\ v^3 - v)$: $\det J = 3v^2 - 1$ vanishes on the lines $v = \pm 1/\sqrt{3}$. Between them the patch is mirrored, and every output with $|y| \lt 2/(3\sqrt{3}) \approx 0.385$ has three preimages. The grid visibly overlaps itself.
  • z², $(u, v) \mapsto (u^2 - v^2,\ 2uv)$, which is squaring a complex number: $\det J = 4(u^2 + v^2)$, zero only at the origin, where angles double and no local inverse exists.
  • Polar at $r = 0$: a whole edge of the domain collapses to a point.

The converse is false: $\det J \ne 0$ everywhere does not make a map one-to-one globally. Polar coordinates on all of $r > 0$ have $\det J = r > 0$, yet $\theta$ and $\theta + 2\pi$ land on the same point.

8. The chain rule multiplies determinants

Doing $\mathbf{f}$ and then $\mathbf{g}$ composes their local linear maps, so the Jacobians multiply as matrices, and their determinants multiply as numbers:

$$J_{\mathbf{g}\circ\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{p}) = J_{\mathbf{g}}\big(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{p})\big)\,J_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{p}), \qquad \det J_{\mathbf{g}\circ\mathbf{f}} = \det J_{\mathbf{g}} \cdot \det J_{\mathbf{f}}$$

Area scaled by one factor and then by another is scaled by the product. Two consequences: a map composed with its inverse has determinant $1$, which gives the inverse rule of section 5; and a map built from area-preserving steps preserves area. The "Swirl" map rotates each circle by an angle that grows with its radius. It twists shapes badly, yet $\det J = 1$ everywhere: in polar terms it is a shear, $(r, \theta) \mapsto (r,\ \theta + k r^2)$.

9. Probability densities

A density is probability per unit area. If $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})$ is one-to-one and smooth, probability is conserved, but areas change by $|\det J|$, so density changes by the inverse factor:

$$p_Y(\mathbf{y}) = p_X(\mathbf{x})\,\frac{1}{\left|\det J_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x})\right|}, \qquad \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{f}^{-1}(\mathbf{y})$$

In one dimension: if $X$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$ and $Y = 2X$, then $Y$ is uniform on $[0, 2]$ with density $\tfrac{1}{2}$. The values are spread over twice the length, so they are half as dense.

  • Box–Muller. Two uniform numbers $U_1, U_2$ become two independent standard normals via $R = \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}$, $\Theta = 2\pi U_2$, $(Z_1, Z_2) = (R\cos\Theta,\ R\sin\Theta)$. The polar Jacobian $r$ is what makes the result Gaussian.
  • Normalizing flows. Generative models push a simple density through a chain of invertible neural maps. Training maximizes $\log p_X(\mathbf{x}) = \log p_Z(\mathbf{z}) + \log\left|\det \frac{\partial \mathbf{z}}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\right|$, so the layers are designed to have triangular Jacobians, whose determinant is just the product of the diagonal.

10. The same thing in code

When the partial derivatives are tedious, approximate them with central differences. The error is about $\epsilon^2$ per entry, so $\epsilon \approx 10^{-5}$ is a good default in double precision.

// f maps [u, v] to [x, y]; returns the 2x2 Jacobian and its determinant
function jacobian(f, u, v, eps = 1e-5) {
  const [xu1, yu1] = f(u + eps, v), [xu0, yu0] = f(u - eps, v);
  const [xv1, yv1] = f(u, v + eps), [xv0, yv0] = f(u, v - eps);
  const J = [[(xu1 - xu0) / (2 * eps), (xv1 - xv0) / (2 * eps)],
             [(yu1 - yu0) / (2 * eps), (yv1 - yv0) / (2 * eps)]];
  return { J, det: J[0][0] * J[1][1] - J[0][1] * J[1][0] };
}

const polar = (r, t) => [r * Math.cos(t), r * Math.sin(t)];
jacobian(polar, 1.5, 0.7).det;   // ≈ 1.5, equal to r

// Area of the unit disk as a sum over a polar grid: sum of |det J| du dv
function diskArea(n) {
  let s = 0;
  const dr = 1 / n, dt = 2 * Math.PI / n;
  for (let i = 0; i < n; i++)
    for (let j = 0; j < n; j++)
      s += Math.abs(jacobian(polar, (i + 0.5) * dr, (j + 0.5) * dt).det) * dr * dt;
  return s;
}
diskArea(50);   // 3.14159..., while forgetting the factor gives 2π

Automatic differentiation libraries (JAX's jax.jacfwd, PyTorch's torch.autograd.functional.jacobian) compute the exact matrix instead. For large $n$, never expand the determinant by cofactors: use an LU factorization, or better, design the map so the Jacobian is triangular.

11. Common mistakes

  • Writing $dx\,dy = dr\,d\theta$. The factor $r$ is missing; integrating $1$ over the unit disk would give $2\pi$ instead of $\pi$.
  • Dropping the absolute value in the change of variables formula. The worked example's $-\tfrac{1}{2}$ would make an area negative.
  • Using the Jacobian in the wrong direction: multiplying by $\partial(u, v)/\partial(x, y)$ when you need $\partial(x, y)/\partial(u, v)$. They are reciprocals.
  • Changing variables with a map that is not one-to-one on the region, so parts of it are counted twice. The z² map on the full square covers the plane twice.
  • Forgetting to transform the limits of integration along with the integrand.

12. Where it shows up

Integration
change of variables

Polar, cylindrical and spherical coordinates, and any substitution that turns a skewed region into a box.

Probability
density transforms

Box–Muller sampling, the multivariate normal, and normalizing flows all divide by $|\det J|$.

Physics
Liouville

Hamiltonian flow has $\det J = 1$, so volume in phase space is conserved. Incompressible fluids satisfy the same at every instant.

Mechanics
deformation

In continuum mechanics $\det F$ of the deformation gradient is the local volume ratio; $\det F < 0$ would mean matter passing through itself.

Graphics
texture maps

The Jacobian of a UV map tells how much a texture is stretched on screen, which drives mipmap level selection.

Robotics
singularities

A robot arm's joint-to-tool Jacobian with $\det J = 0$ marks a pose where the tool cannot move in some direction.
