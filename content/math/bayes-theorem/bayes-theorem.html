Bayes' Theorem

How to update a belief when new evidence arrives. Start from how common something is, weigh it against how well the evidence points to it, and get the probability you should believe now. It is one line of algebra, and it corrects one of the most common mistakes in reasoning.

1. The idea

  
You start with a belief, the prior. You see some evidence. The evidence is more likely if your belief is true than if it is false, so you raise the belief. The new belief is the posterior. How far it moves depends on two things: how strong the evidence is, and how likely the belief was to begin with.

  
The second part is the one people forget. A strong test for a very rare thing still gives mostly false alarms, because there are so many more healthy people to be wrong about. Try the "Very rare" preset: a test that is right 99.9% of the time, for a condition 1 in 1000 people have, gives a coin flip.

  
New belief = old belief, scaled by how well the evidence fits.






  
2. Hypothesis and evidence

  
Two words carry the whole theorem, so it pays to be exact about them.

  
A hypothesis $H$ is a claim whose probability you want to know. You do not know whether it is true. Examples: this person has the disease, this email is spam, this transaction is fraud, this coin is rigged.

  
Evidence $E$ is something you have actually observed that bears on the claim. For a fraud check, the evidence might be that the transaction comes from an unusual IP address, from a new country, from a device never seen before, or is unusually large, or is one of several in a short time.

  
The hypothesis is what you want to know. The evidence is what you already saw.

  
Textbooks often write the pair as $A$ and $B$ instead of $H$ and $E$. It is the same formula with different letters.


  
A worked example: fraud detection

  
Let $H$ be "this transaction is fraud" and $E$ be "the transaction comes from an unusual IP". The "Fraud check" preset loads these numbers:

  

    
QuantityNameMeaning hereValue

    
      
$P(H)$PriorShare of transactions that are fraud, before looking at the IP0.10

      
$P(E \mid H)$LikelihoodIf it is fraud, chance of an unusual IP0.80

      
$P(E \mid \neg H)$False alarmIf it is honest, chance of an unusual IP anyway0.05

      
$P(E)$EvidenceChance of an unusual IP overallto compute

      
$P(H \mid E)$PosteriorChance of fraud, now that we saw the unusual IPto compute

    
  

  
First find how often an unusual IP shows up at all. It can happen in two ways, through fraud or through an honest transaction:

  
$$P(E)=\underbrace{0.80\times0.10}_{\text{fraud, unusual IP}=0.08}+\underbrace{0.05\times0.90}_{\text{honest, unusual IP}=0.045}=0.125$$

  
Now apply Bayes' theorem:

  
$$P(H \mid E)=\frac{P(E \mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E)}=\frac{0.08}{0.125}=0.64$$

  
Before the evidence the chance of fraud was 10%. After seeing the unusual IP it is 64%. The size of the change came from how much more often fraud produces that IP than honest traffic does: $0.80$ against $0.05$, a factor of 16.

  
Notice the denominator. Of every 1000 transactions, 100 are fraud and 80 of those come from an unusual IP. Of the 900 honest ones, 45 do too. So 125 transactions look like this, and 80 of them are fraud, which is $80/125=64\%$.

The likelihood $P(E \mid H)$ and the posterior $P(H \mid E)$ look alike but ask opposite questions. The first starts from the hypothesis and asks how likely the evidence is. The second starts from the evidence and asks how likely the hypothesis is. Bayes' theorem is the bridge between them.


  
The roles are not fixed

  
Nothing makes a statement a hypothesis or evidence by itself. It depends on the question you are asking:

  
    
    
  • Question one: is this transaction fraud? Hypothesis: "the transaction is fraud". Evidence: "it came from an unusual IP".
    • 
    
  • Question two: has this account been hacked? Hypothesis: "the account is compromised". Evidence: "there was a login from an unusual IP".
    • 
  

  
The data is nearly the same, but the hypothesis differs, so the priors and likelihoods differ, and so does the answer. Always begin by writing down the hypothesis in one sentence.






  
3. The formula

  
Let $H$ be the hypothesis and $E$ the evidence. The chance of both happening can be written two ways:

  
$$P(H \cap E) = P(E \mid H)\,P(H) = P(H \mid E)\,P(E)$$

  
Divide both sides by $P(E)$ and you get Bayes' theorem:

  
$$P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E)}$$

  
The bottom part, $P(E)$, is all the ways the evidence can show up. Split it into two cases, with the hypothesis true and false. This is the law of total probability:

  
$$P(E) = P(E \mid H)\,P(H) + P(E \mid \neg H)\,P(\neg H)$$

The area picture

  
Switch Fig 1 to "Area". The square is everyone, total area 1. The left strip, of width $P(H)$, is the people with the condition; the right strip is the rest. Inside each strip, the shaded height is the chance of a positive result: $P(E \mid H)$ on the left, $P(E \mid \neg H)$ on the right. So the shaded orange area is $P(E \mid H)P(H)$, the shaded blue area is $P(E \mid \neg H)P(\neg H)$, and together they are $P(E)$. Bayes' theorem is just "orange shaded area divided by all shaded area". Conditioning on $E$ means throwing away everything unshaded.






  
4. Count people instead

  
The formula is easier to trust when you count. Take the default sliders: 1% of people have the condition, the test catches 90% of them, and it falsely flags 9% of the rest. Out of 1000 people:

  
    
    
  • 10 have it, and 9 of them test positive.
    • 
    
  • 990 do not, and about 89 of them test positive anyway.
    • 
    
  • So 98 people test positive, and only 9 of them have it.
    • 
  

  
A positive result means about $9 / 98 \approx 9\%$, not 90%. That is the same number the formula gives. Counting in whole people like this is called using natural frequencies, and studies of doctors and juries find it cuts reasoning errors dramatically compared with the same problem stated in percentages.

6. Base rate neglect

  
People often answer with the hit rate, 90%, because it is the number the test advertises. That swaps $P(E \mid H)$ for $P(H \mid E)$. They are different questions: "how many sick people test positive" and "how many positive people are sick".

  
The left plot in Fig 2 shows the whole picture for the current test. The orange curve is the posterior after a positive result for every possible base rate. It starts near zero, so a positive result for a rare condition is still probably a false alarm, and rises steeply once the condition is common. The test did not change. Only the population did. The blue curve is the same for a negative result, and the diagonal is "the evidence changed nothing".

  
A practical consequence: screening the whole population and testing people who already show symptoms are different situations, even with the identical test, because the symptoms have already raised the prior.






  
7. Updating again and again

  
Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior. In odds form, each piece of evidence just multiplies the odds by a fixed number, the likelihood ratio:

  
$$\underbrace{\frac{P(H \mid E)}{P(\neg H \mid E)}}_{\text{posterior odds}} = \underbrace{\frac{P(H)}{P(\neg H)}}_{\text{prior odds}} \times \underbrace{\frac{P(E \mid H)}{P(E \mid \neg H)}}_{\text{likelihood ratio}}$$

  
With the defaults the ratio is $0.90 / 0.09 = 10$. Prior odds of about $1 : 99$ become about $10 : 99$ after one positive test, which is roughly 9%. A second independent positive test multiplies by 10 again, giving about $100 : 99$, or roughly 50%. Use the buttons under the right plot of Fig 2 to run this yourself.


  
How the new prior is set

  
After each piece of evidence, the new prior is simply the posterior you just computed. Nothing else is added or adjusted:

  
$$P_{\text{new}}(H) = P(H \mid E_1) = \frac{P(E_1 \mid H)\,P_{\text{old}}(H)}{P(E_1 \mid H)\,P_{\text{old}}(H) + P(E_1 \mid \neg H)\,\bigl(1-P_{\text{old}}(H)\bigr)}$$

$P(E)$ changes every round, because it depends on the prior. The hit rate and false alarm rate stay the same, since they describe the test, not your belief. A negative result updates the same way, but uses the complementary likelihoods $P(\neg E \mid H) = 0.10$ and $P(\neg E \mid \neg H) = 0.91$, a ratio of about $0.11$. The belief goes down instead of up.

  
Two conditions must hold: each new piece of evidence must be a fresh observation, and the pieces must be independent given $H$. Counting the same evidence twice, or treating two linked signals as independent, pushes the belief too far.


  
Many pieces of evidence at once

  
With conditional independence (once you know whether $H$ is true, one piece of evidence tells you nothing new about another), the joint likelihood is a product and every piece multiplies the odds by its own ratio:

  
$$\frac{P(H \mid E_1,\dots,E_n)}{P(\neg H \mid E_1,\dots,E_n)}=\frac{P(H)}{P(\neg H)}\times\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{P(E_i \mid H)}{P(E_i \mid \neg H)}$$

  
Take the logarithm and the product turns into a sum. Each piece of evidence adds or subtracts a fixed amount of "weight" to the log-odds. This is exactly what a naive Bayes classifier does:

  
$$\ln\frac{P(H \mid E_1,\dots,E_n)}{P(\neg H \mid E_1,\dots,E_n)}=\ln\frac{P(H)}{P(\neg H)}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln\frac{P(E_i \mid H)}{P(E_i \mid \neg H)}$$

  
To get the probability back from odds $O$, use $P = \dfrac{O}{1+O}$.

8. More than two hypotheses

  
Nothing in the derivation needs $H$ to be a yes-or-no question. With competing hypotheses $H_1, \dots, H_k$ that are mutually exclusive and cover every case, the evidence term sums over all of them:

  
$$P(H_i \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H_i)\,P(H_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{k} P(E \mid H_j)\,P(H_j)}$$

  
A factory has three machines. A, B and C make 50%, 30% and 20% of the parts, with defect rates 1%, 2% and 3%. A part is defective. Which machine made it?

A practical shortcut: compute prior times likelihood for every hypothesis, then divide each by the total so they add up to 1. You often do not need $P(E)$ at all, only the ranking, which is why people write $P(H \mid E) \propto P(E \mid H)\,P(H)$.






  
9. Two famous traps

  
The prosecutor's fallacy

  
A DNA profile found at a crime scene occurs in 1 in a million people. A suspect matches. The prosecutor says: "there is a one in a million chance the defendant is innocent." That is $P(\text{match} \mid \text{innocent})$ presented as $P(\text{innocent} \mid \text{match})$, the same swap as base rate neglect.

  
In a city of 10 million, about 10 innocent people would also match. If nothing else points to the suspect, the match alone makes guilt about $\frac{1}{11} \approx 9\%$. The match is strong evidence (a likelihood ratio of a million) but it has to be combined with a prior, and a random person's prior of guilt is tiny. Real cases add other evidence, which is how the posterior becomes convincing.

  
Monty Hall

  
Three doors, one car. You pick door 1. The host, who knows where the car is and always opens a door with a goat, opens door 3. Should you switch to door 2?

  
$$P(\text{car}_2 \mid \text{opens}_3) = \frac{P(\text{opens}_3 \mid \text{car}_2)\,P(\text{car}_2)}{P(\text{opens}_3)} = \frac{1 \cdot \frac{1}{3}}{\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{2}{3}$$

  
If the car is behind door 2, the host is forced to open door 3, likelihood $1$. If it is behind door 1, he picks door 2 or 3 at random, likelihood $\frac{1}{2}$. If it is behind door 3 he cannot open it, likelihood $0$. So $P(\text{opens}_3) = \frac{1}{3}(\frac{1}{2} + 1 + 0) = \frac{1}{2}$, and switching wins two times in three. The host's choice is evidence, because it depends on where the car is.






  
10. From events to parameters

  
So far the hypothesis was a single yes-or-no statement. Bayesian statistics applies the same formula to an unknown number, such as the probability $\theta$ that a coin lands heads. The prior and posterior become whole distributions over $\theta$:

  
$$p(\theta \mid \text{data}) = \frac{p(\text{data} \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)}{\int p(\text{data} \mid \theta')\,p(\theta')\,d\theta'} \;\propto\; p(\text{data} \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)$$

  
For a coin the algebra is especially clean. Start with a Beta prior, $p(\theta) \propto \theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}$. Observe $h$ heads and $t$ tails; the likelihood is $\theta^h(1-\theta)^t$. Multiply and the posterior is again a Beta, with the counts simply added:

  
$$\text{Beta}(a, b) \;\xrightarrow{\;h \text{ heads},\ t \text{ tails}\;}\; \text{Beta}(a + h,\ b + t)$$

  
With a flat prior $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$ and 7 heads in 10 flips, the posterior is $\text{Beta}(8, 4)$. Its mean is $\frac{8}{12} \approx 0.67$, a little closer to $\frac{1}{2}$ than the raw $0.7$, because the prior counts as two imaginary flips. With 700 heads in 1000 flips the prior barely matters. That is the general pattern: with little data the prior matters, with lots of data the evidence dominates.






  
11. The same thing in code

  
function posterior(prior, hit, falseAlarm, positive = true) {
  const lh = positive ? hit : 1 - hit;              // P(result | H)
  const ln = positive ? falseAlarm : 1 - falseAlarm; // P(result | not H)
  return (lh * prior) / (lh * prior + ln * (1 - prior));
}

posterior(0.01, 0.90, 0.09);          // 0.092   (positive)
posterior(0.01, 0.90, 0.09, false);   // 0.0011  (negative)

// Many results at once, in log-odds (assumes independence given H)
function posteriorMany(prior, results, hit, falseAlarm) {
  let logOdds = Math.log(prior / (1 - prior));
  for (const positive of results) {
    logOdds += positive ? Math.log(hit / falseAlarm)
                        : Math.log((1 - hit) / (1 - falseAlarm));
  }
  return 1 / (1 + Math.exp(-logOdds));          // odds back to probability
}

posteriorMany(0.01, [true, true, true], 0.90, 0.09);   // 0.910
posteriorMany(0.01, [true, true, false], 0.90, 0.09);  // 0.100

  
Working in log-odds is not just tidy. Multiplying hundreds of small likelihoods underflows to zero in floating point; adding their logarithms does not. Spam filters that weigh thousands of words rely on this.






  
12. Common mistakes

  
    
    
  • Confusing $P(E \mid H)$ with $P(H \mid E)$. "Most terrorists are young men" does not mean most young men are terrorists.
    • 
    
  • Ignoring the base rate, or using one from the wrong population.
    • 
    
  • Treating correlated evidence as independent, for example two tests that fail for the same biological reason.
    • 
    
  • Using a prior of exactly 0 or 1. No evidence can move it, since every likelihood ratio gets multiplied by zero or infinity.
    • 
    
  • Forgetting the false-alarm term in the denominator, which gives $P(E \mid H)P(H)$ instead of a probability.
    • 
  






  
13. Where it shows up

  

    
Email
spam filters
Each word nudges the log-odds of spam. Naive Bayes was the first filter that worked at scale.

    
Medicine
screening
Reading a test result against prevalence decides whether to retest, treat or reassure.

    
Robotics
filters
Kalman and particle filters alternate predict and Bayes-update steps as sensor readings arrive.

    
Machine learning
inference
Bayesian neural networks, Gaussian processes and Bayesian optimization all keep a posterior over unknowns.

    
A/B testing
Beta posteriors
"Probability that B beats A" comes straight from two Beta posteriors over conversion rates.

    
Data structures
Bloom filters
A Bloom filter says "maybe present". Bayes' theorem says how much to trust that answer.

  

  
The result is only as good as the inputs. A wrong base rate or independence that does not hold will give a confident, wrong answer.
