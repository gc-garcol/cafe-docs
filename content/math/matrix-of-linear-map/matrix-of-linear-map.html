Matrix of a Linear Map

A linear map is a geometric action. A matrix is that action written in a chosen coordinate system. Change the basis and the same map gets a new matrix.

Try it first

Pick a map $T$ and a basis $\mathcal{B} = (\mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2)$. The faint lines are the standard grid and the colored lines are the grid of $\mathcal{B}$. The matrix $[T]_{\mathcal{B}}$ has as its columns the coordinates of $T(\mathbf{b}_1)$ and $T(\mathbf{b}_2)$ measured in $\mathcal{B}$. With the starting choice, the reflection becomes the diagonal matrix with entries $1$ and $-1$.

1. Building the matrix

  
Let $T: V \to V$ be linear and let $\mathcal{B} = (\mathbf{b}_1, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n)$ be a basis. Because $T$ is linear, it is fully decided by what it does to the basis vectors. Write each image in the same basis:

  
$$T(\mathbf{b}_j) = m_{1j}\mathbf{b}_1 + m_{2j}\mathbf{b}_2 + \cdots + m_{nj}\mathbf{b}_n$$

  
The numbers $m_{ij}$ fill the matrix, one column per basis vector:

  
$$[T]_{\mathcal{B}} = \Big[\, [T(\mathbf{b}_1)]_{\mathcal{B}} \;\; [T(\mathbf{b}_2)]_{\mathcal{B}} \;\cdots\; [T(\mathbf{b}_n)]_{\mathcal{B}} \,\Big]$$

  
It works the way you would hope: $[T(\mathbf{x})]_{\mathcal{B}} = [T]_{\mathcal{B}}\,[\mathbf{x}]_{\mathcal{B}}$. The matrix acts on coordinates.

  
Columns of the matrix are the images of the basis vectors, written in the same basis.






  
2. The standard matrix

  
On $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the standard basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$, coordinates are the entries themselves, so the columns are simply $T(e_1), \dots, T(e_n)$. For the rotation by $\theta$ in the plane:

  
$$T(e_1) = \begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta \\ \sin\theta\end{bmatrix},\quad T(e_2) = \begin{bmatrix}-\sin\theta \\ \cos\theta\end{bmatrix} \;\Longrightarrow\; [T] = \begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{bmatrix}$$






  
3. Changing the basis

  
Let $A$ be the standard matrix and $P$ the matrix whose columns are the new basis vectors $\mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2$. Then $P$ turns $\mathcal{B}$-coordinates into standard coordinates, and $P^{-1}$ does the reverse. To apply $T$ in new coordinates, convert to standard, apply $A$, and convert back:

  
$$[T]_{\mathcal{B}} = P^{-1} A\, P$$

  
Matrices related this way are called similar. They describe the same map, so they share the determinant, the rank, the trace and the eigenvalues. The basis only changes how the numbers look.

4. Why bother

  
Some bases make the matrix much simpler. In the lab, the reflection over the line $y = x$ has the standard matrix $\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$. In the basis $(1,1), (-1,1)$, one vector lies on the mirror and stays put, and the other is exactly flipped, so the matrix is $\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}$. A diagonal matrix is easy to read, to multiply and to raise to powers. Finding such a basis is the job of diagonalization.






  
5. Different spaces

  
The same recipe works when input and output spaces differ, with a basis for each. For $T: V \to W$ with bases $\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{C}$, the matrix $[T]_{\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}}$ has columns $[T(\mathbf{b}_j)]_{\mathcal{C}}$. It has size $\dim W \times \dim V$. For example, differentiation on polynomials of degree at most 2, with basis $(1, x, x^2)$, is the matrix:

  
$$\frac{d}{dx} \;\longleftrightarrow\; \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

  
Its columns are the derivatives of $1$, $x$ and $x^2$, which are $0$, $1$ and $2x$.
