Vector Spaces

A set where you can add things and stretch them by numbers, with the usual rules holding. Arrows, polynomials, matrices and functions all fit, so one theory covers them all.

Try it first

Two vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ span every combination $\alpha\mathbf{u} + \beta\mathbf{v}$. The grey dots are a sample of those combinations. If $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ point in different directions, the dots fill the plane, they form a basis, and the target $\mathbf{w}$ gets exact coordinates $(\alpha, \beta)$. Make them line up and the span collapses to a line.

1. The idea

  
A vector space over the real numbers is a set $V$ with two operations: add two elements, and multiply an element by a number. They must obey eight rules, so that ordinary algebra works.

  

    
GroupRules

    
      
Addition$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}$. $(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})$. A zero vector $\mathbf{0}$ with $\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{v}$. Every $\mathbf{v}$ has an opposite $-\mathbf{v}$.

      
Scaling$1\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$ and $a(b\mathbf{v}) = (ab)\mathbf{v}$.

      
Together$a(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = a\mathbf{u} + a\mathbf{v}$ and $(a + b)\mathbf{v} = a\mathbf{v} + b\mathbf{v}$.

    
  

  
The elements do not have to look like arrows. Anything that follows the rules is a vector.

2. Subspaces

  
A subset $W \subseteq V$ is a subspace if it is itself a vector space. Three checks are enough:

  
    
    
  • $\mathbf{0}$ is in $W$.
    • 
    
  • If $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are in $W$, so is $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$.
    • 
    
  • If $\mathbf{v}$ is in $W$ and $c$ is a number, so is $c\mathbf{v}$.
    • 
  

  
In the plane, the subspaces are the origin alone, any line through the origin, and the whole plane. A line that misses the origin fails the first check. The null space and column space from Matrix Rank are subspaces.






  
3. Span, independence, basis

  
The span of some vectors is the set of all their linear combinations. The vectors are linearly independent if none is a combination of the others. Equivalently, the only way to combine them into zero is with every coefficient zero:

  
$$c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0} \;\Longrightarrow\; c_1 = c_2 = \cdots = c_k = 0$$

  
A basis is an independent list that spans the whole space. Every basis of a space has the same number of vectors, and that number is the dimension. In the lab, two vectors in the plane form a basis exactly when $u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1 \neq 0$. That quantity is the $2 \times 2$ determinant.

  
A basis is a coordinate system: no wasted vectors, and nothing left out.






  
4. Coordinates

  
Fix a basis $\mathcal{B} = (\mathbf{b}_1, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n)$. Every vector can be written in exactly one way as a combination of it, and those coefficients are its coordinates.

  
$$\mathbf{w} = \alpha_1\mathbf{b}_1 + \cdots + \alpha_n\mathbf{b}_n \;\Longleftrightarrow\; [\mathbf{w}]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha_1 \\ \vdots \\ \alpha_n \end{bmatrix}$$

  
The same vector has different coordinates in different bases. Choosing a good basis is half of linear algebra, and it is the idea behind the matrix of a linear map and diagonalization.






  
5. Where it shows up

  

    
Signals
Fourier basis
Sines and cosines are a basis for periodic functions. The coordinates are the Fourier coefficients.

    
Data
features
A data point is a vector. Principal components pick a better basis for the data.

    
Differential equations
solution space
Solutions of a linear equation form a vector space. A basis of it generates every solution.
