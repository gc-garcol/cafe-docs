Vector Spaces

A set where you can add things and stretch them by numbers, with the usual rules holding. Arrows, polynomials, matrices and functions all fit, so one theory covers them all. Its key ideas are span, independence, basis and dimension, and they all reduce to one question: which combinations can you make?

1. The idea

A vector space over the real numbers is a set $V$ with two operations: add two elements, and multiply an element by a number (a scalar). They must obey eight rules, so that ordinary algebra works.

GroupRules
Addition$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}$. $(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})$. A zero vector $\mathbf{0}$ with $\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{v}$. Every $\mathbf{v}$ has an opposite $-\mathbf{v}$.
Scaling$1\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$ and $a(b\mathbf{v}) = (ab)\mathbf{v}$.
Together$a(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = a\mathbf{u} + a\mathbf{v}$ and $(a + b)\mathbf{v} = a\mathbf{v} + b\mathbf{v}$.

Notice what is missing: length, angle and multiplying two vectors together. A vector space only knows how to combine. The single operation that matters is the linear combination $c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k$.

If you can add it and scale it, and the rules hold, it is a vector.

2. Vectors that are not arrows

Polynomials show how abstract vectors turn into lists of numbers. In the basis $1, x, x^2$, the polynomial $3 - 2x + x^2$ has coordinates $(3, -2, 1)$. Adding polynomials adds their coordinate lists, and scaling scales them. So $\mathcal{P}_2$ behaves exactly like $\mathbb{R}^3$: same structure, different costume. Any vector space of dimension $n$ is a copy of $\mathbb{R}^n$ once you choose a basis.

The last row is why this matters outside geometry. Every solution of $y'' + y = 0$ is $a\cos t + b\sin t$. Find two independent solutions and you have found them all.

3. Subspaces

A subset $W \subseteq V$ is a subspace if it is itself a vector space using the same operations. Three checks are enough:

  • $\mathbf{0}$ is in $W$.
  • Closed under addition: if $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are in $W$, so is $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$.
  • Closed under scaling: if $\mathbf{v}$ is in $W$ and $c$ is any number, so is $c\mathbf{v}$.

Test some candidates below. Pick a set, then change the sample or the scalar $c$ until one of the checks fails, or convince yourself that none can.

In the plane, the only subspaces are the origin alone, lines through the origin, and the whole plane. In $\mathbb{R}^3$ add planes through the origin. The pattern to remember: solution sets of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ are always subspaces (the null space), while solution sets of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ with $\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{0}$ never are, since they miss the origin. They are shifted copies of the null space.

4. Span

The span of some vectors is the set of all their linear combinations. It is always a subspace: the smallest one that contains them.

$$\operatorname{span}(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k) = \{\, c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k : c_1, \dots, c_k \in \mathbb{R} \,\}$$

In $\mathbb{R}^3$: one nonzero vector spans a line, two non-parallel vectors span a plane, and three vectors not in one plane span all of space. In the lab, "Dependent" shows two vectors whose span is only a line. Asking whether $\mathbf{w}$ is in the span of the columns of $A$ is the same as asking whether $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{w}$ has a solution.

5. Linear independence

Vectors are linearly independent if none of them is a combination of the others. Equivalently, the only way to combine them into zero is with every coefficient zero:

$$c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0} \;\Longrightarrow\; c_1 = c_2 = \cdots = c_k = 0$$

Dependent vectors carry redundant information: one of them adds nothing to the span. Two ways to test:

  • $n$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$: put them as columns of a matrix. They are independent exactly when its determinant is not zero. In the lab that is $u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1 \neq 0$.
  • Any number of vectors: row-reduce the matrix. They are independent when every column gets a pivot, that is, when the rank equals the number of vectors.
  1. Are $(1, 0, 1)$, $(0, 1, 1)$, $(1, 1, 2)$ independent?
  2. $\det\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = 1(2 - 1) - 0 + 1(0 - 1) = 0$. No.
  3. Indeed the third is the sum of the first two. They span only a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

More than $n$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ are always dependent: there is no room for a new direction.

6. Basis and dimension

A basis is an independent list that spans the whole space: enough vectors to reach everything, and none to spare.

A basis is a coordinate system: no wasted vectors, and nothing left out.

A space has many bases, but they all have the same number of vectors. That number is the dimension. The reason, called the exchange lemma, is that you can swap the vectors of one basis into another one at a time without ever losing the span, so neither list can be longer.

Two shortcuts follow. In a space of dimension $n$, any $n$ independent vectors automatically span, and any $n$ vectors that span are automatically independent. So for $n$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ you only need to check one of the two conditions.

7. Coordinates and change of basis

Fix a basis $\mathcal{B} = (\mathbf{b}_1, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n)$. Every vector can be written in exactly one way as a combination of it, and those coefficients are its coordinates.

$$\mathbf{w} = \alpha_1\mathbf{b}_1 + \cdots + \alpha_n\mathbf{b}_n \;\Longleftrightarrow\; [\mathbf{w}]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha_1 \\ \vdots \\ \alpha_n \end{bmatrix}$$

Existence comes from spanning, uniqueness from independence. If two combinations gave the same $\mathbf{w}$, subtracting them would give a nontrivial combination equal to zero.

To compute coordinates, put the basis vectors as columns of $P = [\,\mathbf{b}_1 \cdots \mathbf{b}_n\,]$. Then $\mathbf{w} = P\,[\mathbf{w}]_{\mathcal{B}}$, so $[\mathbf{w}]_{\mathcal{B}} = P^{-1}\mathbf{w}$. With the lab's starting values:

$$\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},\ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix},\ \mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}: \quad \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

So $\mathbf{w} = 2\mathbf{u} + 1\mathbf{v}$. In the standard basis $\mathbf{w}$ is $(3, 4)$; in the basis $(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})$ the same arrow is $(2, 1)$. Choosing a good basis is half of linear algebra. It is the idea behind the matrix of a linear map and diagonalization.

8. Orthogonal bases make coordinates cheap

Once you add a dot product, some bases are much nicer than others. If the basis vectors are mutually perpendicular, each coordinate is a single dot product, no inverse needed:

$$\alpha_i = \frac{\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{b}_i}{\mathbf{b}_i \cdot \mathbf{b}_i}$$

The lab's starting basis is orthogonal, since $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = -2 + 2 = 0$. So $\alpha = \frac{6 + 4}{5} = 2$ and $\beta = \frac{-3 + 8}{5} = 1$, matching section 7. The readout shows this shortcut whenever $\mathbf{u} \perp \mathbf{v}$. If the vectors also have length 1 the basis is orthonormal and the denominators disappear. The Gram–Schmidt process turns any basis into an orthonormal one by subtracting projections, one vector at a time.

9. The subspaces of a matrix

Every $m \times n$ matrix $A$ comes with subspaces that answer the basic questions about $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$.

SubspaceDefinitionLives inDimension
Column spaceall $A\mathbf{x}$: the reachable $\mathbf{b}$$\mathbb{R}^m$$r$ (the rank)
Null spaceall $\mathbf{x}$ with $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$$\mathbb{R}^n$$n - r$
Row spacespan of the rows$\mathbb{R}^n$$r$
Left null spaceall $\mathbf{y}$ with $A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$$\mathbb{R}^m$$m - r$

The rank–nullity theorem ties them together: $\dim(\text{column space}) + \dim(\text{null space}) = n$. Every input direction either survives into the output or is crushed to zero. For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$ the rank is $1$ (the rows are proportional), so the null space is a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$: $1 + 2 = 3$.

10. The same thing in code

Independence and dimension come down to counting pivots after row reduction.

function rank(vectors, tol = 1e-10) {
  const A = vectors.map(v => v.slice());   // each vector is a row
  let r = 0;
  for (let col = 0; col < A[0].length && r < A.length; col++) {
    let p = r;
    for (let i = r + 1; i < A.length; i++) if (Math.abs(A[i][col]) > Math.abs(A[p][col])) p = i;
    if (Math.abs(A[p][col]) < tol) continue;      // no pivot in this column
    [A[p], A[r]] = [A[r], A[p]];
    for (let i = r + 1; i < A.length; i++) {
      const m = A[i][col] / A[r][col];
      for (let k = col; k < A[0].length; k++) A[i][k] -= m * A[r][k];
    }
    r++;
  }
  return r;   // = dimension of the span
}

rank([[1, 0, 1], [0, 1, 1], [1, 1, 2]]);  // 2: dependent, they span a plane
rank([[1, 0, 0], [1, 1, 0], [1, 1, 1]]);  // 3: a basis of R^3

The tolerance matters. With measured data, vectors are almost never exactly dependent, so "nearly dependent" is the real question. The singular value decomposition answers it properly by measuring how close the vectors come to a lower-dimensional subspace.

11. Common mistakes

  • Calling a line or plane that misses the origin a subspace. It fails the zero check.
  • Thinking a union of subspaces is a subspace. The two axes together are not closed under addition.
  • Checking independence pairwise. Three vectors can be pairwise non-parallel and still lie in one plane.
  • Saying "the basis" of a space. There are infinitely many; only the dimension is fixed.
  • Forgetting that coordinates depend on the basis. $(2, 1)$ means nothing until you say "in which basis".

12. Where it shows up

Signals
Fourier basis

Sines and cosines form an orthogonal basis for periodic functions. The coordinates are the Fourier coefficients, each one a single inner product.

Data
PCA

A data point is a vector of features. Principal components pick an orthonormal basis that puts most of the variance in the first few coordinates.

Differential equations
solution space

Solutions of a linear homogeneous equation form a vector space. A basis of it generates every solution.

Graphics
frames

Each object, camera and bone has its own basis. Rendering is a chain of coordinate changes between them.

Machine learning
embeddings

Words and images become vectors, and meaning is read from linear combinations and directions in that space.

Coding theory
linear codes

Error-correcting codes are subspaces over the field with two elements. The same rules apply with $1 + 1 = 0$.
