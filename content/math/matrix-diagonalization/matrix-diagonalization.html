Matrix Diagonalization

Pick a basis made of eigenvectors, and a matrix turns into a plain list of stretch factors. Powers, exponentials and recurrences then become one line of arithmetic.

Diagonalizable?

    

  






  
1. The idea

  
A square matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if it is similar to a diagonal matrix. That means there is an invertible $P$ and a diagonal $D$ with:

  
$$A = P D P^{-1} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad D = P^{-1} A P$$

  
This is the change of basis formula with a good choice of basis. The columns of $P$ are eigenvectors of $A$, and the diagonal of $D$ holds the matching eigenvalues, in the same order. In the eigenvector basis the map is just "scale each coordinate".

  
$A$ is diagonalizable exactly when it has $n$ independent eigenvectors.






  
2. How to do it

  
    
    
  • Solve $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ to get the eigenvalues.
    • 
    
  • For each eigenvalue, solve $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ to get its eigenvectors.
    • 
    
  • If you have $n$ independent eigenvectors in total, put them in the columns of $P$ and the eigenvalues, in the same order, on the diagonal of $D$.
    • 
  

  
Worked example. Take the matrix from the first preset.

  
$$A = \begin{bmatrix}4 & 1\\ 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}: \quad \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \lambda = 5,\; 2$$

  
For $\lambda = 5$: $(A - 5I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ gives $\mathbf{v} = (1, 1)$. For $\lambda = 2$: $\mathbf{v} = (1, -2)$. So:

  
$$P = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & -2\end{bmatrix},\quad D = \begin{bmatrix}5 & 0\\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix},\quad A = P D P^{-1}$$

  
If two eigenvalues are different, their eigenvectors are automatically independent. So $n$ distinct eigenvalues always give a diagonalizable matrix. Repeated eigenvalues need a check.






  
3. Why it pays off: powers

  
The middle factors cancel when you multiply $A$ by itself:

  
$$A^n = (PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1})\cdots(PDP^{-1}) = P D^n P^{-1}, \qquad D^n = \begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1^n & 0\\ 0 & \lambda_2^n\end{bmatrix}$$

  
For the example, $A^n = P\,\operatorname{diag}(5^n, 2^n)\,P^{-1}$, with no need to multiply $n$ times. The same trick works for any function that has a power series, for instance the matrix exponential $e^{A} = P e^{D} P^{-1}$, where $e^D$ just exponentiates the diagonal. That is how linear differential equations $\mathbf{x}' = A\mathbf{x}$ are solved.

  
It also explains the long run behavior in the lab. Write a starting vector in the eigenbasis and apply $A^n$: each part gets multiplied by $\lambda_i^n$. The one with the largest $|\lambda|$ eventually dominates, so every direction bends toward its eigenvector.

  
A classic use is the Fibonacci numbers. Since $\begin{bmatrix}F_{n+1}\\ F_n\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}^n\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}$, diagonalizing gives Binet's formula with the eigenvalues $\varphi = \frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}$ and $\frac{1-\sqrt5}{2}$.






  
4. When it fails

  

    
CaseExampleWhat goes wrong

    
      
Defective$\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$Eigenvalue $1$ is repeated, but there is only one eigen direction. Too few eigenvectors for $P$.

      
Complex eigenvalues$\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$No real eigenvectors. Over the complex numbers it does diagonalize, with $\pm i$.

    
  

  
A defective matrix can still be simplified to a Jordan form, which has some 1s above the diagonal. The shear preset shows that: the ellipse stays tilted and never picks a second axis.






  
5. The best case: symmetric matrices

  
The spectral theorem says that a real symmetric matrix always has real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors. Normalize those to length 1 and $P$ becomes orthogonal, so $P^{-1} = P^{\mathsf T}$:

  
$$A = Q \Lambda Q^{\mathsf T}, \qquad Q^{\mathsf T} Q = I$$

  
No inversion is needed, and the numbers stay stable. Principal component analysis is this theorem applied to a covariance matrix. Try the Symmetric preset: the two dashed lines cross at a right angle.






  
6. Where it shows up

  

    
Dynamics
$A^n$
Repeated application of a rule, like population models and Markov chains, reduces to $\lambda^n$.

    
Differential equations
$e^{At}$
Linear systems have solutions built from eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

    
Data
PCA
Diagonalizing a covariance matrix finds the axes of greatest variance.
