Matrix Diagonalization

Pick a basis made of eigenvectors, and a matrix turns into a plain list of stretch factors. Powers, exponentials and recurrences then become one line of arithmetic.

Diagonalizable?

    

  






  
1. The idea

  
A square matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if it is similar to a diagonal matrix. That means there is an invertible $P$ and a diagonal $D$ with:

  
$$A = P D P^{-1} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad D = P^{-1} A P$$

  
This is the change of basis formula with a good choice of basis. The columns of $P$ are eigenvectors of $A$, and the diagonal of $D$ holds the matching eigenvalues, in the same order. In the eigenvector basis the map is just "scale each coordinate".

  
Why the columns must be eigenvectors: multiply $A = PDP^{-1}$ on the right by $P$ to get $AP = PD$. Column $i$ of $AP$ is $A\mathbf{v}_i$, and column $i$ of $PD$ is $\lambda_i \mathbf{v}_i$. So $AP = PD$ is just the $n$ equations $A\mathbf{v}_i = \lambda_i \mathbf{v}_i$ written side by side, and $P$ is invertible exactly when those $\mathbf{v}_i$ are independent.

  
$A$ is diagonalizable exactly when it has $n$ independent eigenvectors.






  
2. Read it right to left: three moves

  
Apply $A = PDP^{-1}$ to a vector $\mathbf{x}$ and read the product from right to left:

  
    
    
  1. $P^{-1}\mathbf{x}$ rewrites $\mathbf{x}$ in eigen coordinates: the numbers $c_1, c_2$ with $\mathbf{x} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2$.
    2. 
    
  2. $D$ scales each coordinate on its own: $c_1 \to \lambda_1 c_1$, $c_2 \to \lambda_2 c_2$. Nothing mixes.
    3. 
    
  3. $P$ turns the scaled coordinates back into an ordinary vector: $\lambda_1 c_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + \lambda_2 c_2 \mathbf{v}_2 = A\mathbf{x}$.
    4. 
  

  
Fig 2 uses the matrix from the lab above. On the left, $\mathbf{x}$ is split along the two eigen directions, and each piece is stretched by its own eigenvalue. On the right is the same thing in eigen coordinates. There the slanted parallelogram becomes a plain rectangle, and $A$ is nothing but the diagonal matrix $D$.


  
  

    

    

  






  
3. How to do it

  
    
    
  1. Solve $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ to get the eigenvalues.
    2. 
    
  2. For each eigenvalue, solve $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ to get its eigenvectors.
    3. 
    
  3. Count them. If you have $n$ independent eigenvectors in total, put them in the columns of $P$ and the eigenvalues, in the same order, on the diagonal of $D$. If you have fewer, stop: $A$ is not diagonalizable.
    4. 
  

  
Worked example. Take the matrix from the first preset.

  
$$A = \begin{bmatrix}4 & 1\\ 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}: \quad \det(A - \lambda I) = (4-\lambda)(3-\lambda) - 2 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \lambda = 5,\; 2$$

  
For $\lambda = 5$: $A - 5I = \begin{bmatrix}-1 & 1\\ 2 & -2\end{bmatrix}$, so $-v_1 + v_2 = 0$ and $\mathbf{v} = (1, 1)$. For $\lambda = 2$: $A - 2I = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 1\\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}$, so $2v_1 + v_2 = 0$ and $\mathbf{v} = (1, -2)$. So:

  
$$P = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & -2\end{bmatrix},\quad D = \begin{bmatrix}5 & 0\\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix},\quad P^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1\\ 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}$$

  
Check: $PD = \begin{bmatrix}5 & 2\\ 5 & -4\end{bmatrix}$, and $PD \cdot P^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}12 & 3\\ 6 & 9\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4 & 1\\ 2 & 3\end{bmatrix} = A$. Always do this check; a sign slip in one eigenvector shows up at once.

  
If two eigenvalues are different, their eigenvectors are automatically independent. So $n$ distinct eigenvalues always give a diagonalizable matrix. Repeated eigenvalues need the check in the next section.






  
4. Repeated eigenvalues: count the eigenvectors

  
Each eigenvalue has two counts:

  
    
    
  • Algebraic multiplicity: how many times $\lambda$ is a root of $\det(A - \lambda I)$.
    • 
    
  • Geometric multiplicity: how many independent eigenvectors it has, which is $\dim \ker(A - \lambda I) = n - \operatorname{rank}(A - \lambda I)$.
    • 
  

  
The geometric count is always at least 1 and at most the algebraic count. $A$ is diagonalizable exactly when the two counts are equal for every eigenvalue (and all eigenvalues are real, if you want a real $P$).

  

    
MatrixEigenvalueAlgebraicGeometricDiagonalizable?

    
      
$\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}$$2$22yes, it already is diagonal

      
$\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{bmatrix}$$2$21no

      
$\begin{bmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{bmatrix}$$5,\ 2$1 each1 eachyes

    
  

  
For $2 \times 2$ matrices there is a shortcut: a repeated eigenvalue $\lambda$ gives a diagonalizable matrix only if $A = \lambda I$. Anything else with a repeated eigenvalue is defective.






  
5. Why it pays off: powers

  
The middle factors cancel when you multiply $A$ by itself:

  
$$A^n = (PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1})\cdots(PDP^{-1}) = P D^n P^{-1}, \qquad D^n = \begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1^n & 0\\ 0 & \lambda_2^n\end{bmatrix}$$

  
For the example, multiplying out $P\,\operatorname{diag}(5^n, 2^n)\,P^{-1}$ gives a closed formula for every power at once:

  
$$A^n = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}2\cdot 5^n + 2^n & 5^n - 2^n\\ 2\cdot 5^n - 2\cdot 2^n & 5^n + 2\cdot 2^n\end{bmatrix}$$

  
Put in $n = 1$ and you get $A$ back. Put in $n = 3$ and you get $\begin{bmatrix}86 & 39\\ 78 & 47\end{bmatrix}$ without a single matrix product. Computing $A^n$ this way costs one diagonalization plus $n$-th powers of numbers, instead of $n - 1$ matrix multiplications.

  
It also explains the long run behavior in the lab. Write a starting vector in the eigenbasis and apply $A^n$: each part gets multiplied by $\lambda_i^n$. The one with the largest $|\lambda|$ eventually dominates, so every direction bends toward its eigenvector. Here $2^n$ is tiny next to $5^n$, so

  
$$A^n \approx 5^n \cdot \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1\\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix} \quad\text{for large } n,$$

  
and both columns point along $(1, 1)$, the eigenvector for $\lambda = 5$. The ratio $|\lambda_2 / \lambda_1| = 0.4$ tells you how fast the other direction fades. This is the idea behind the power method, which finds the largest eigenvalue by applying $A$ over and over.






  
6. Three classic uses

  
Fibonacci numbers. One step of $F_{n+1} = F_n + F_{n-1}$ is a matrix:

  
$$\begin{bmatrix}F_{n+1}\\ F_n\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}^n\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \lambda^2 - \lambda - 1 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \varphi = \tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2},\; \psi = \tfrac{1-\sqrt5}{2}$$

  
The eigenvectors are $(\varphi, 1)$ and $(\psi, 1)$. Writing $(1, 0)$ in that basis and applying $D^n$ gives Binet's formula:

  
$$F_n = \frac{\varphi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt5}$$

  
Since $|\psi| < 1$, the second term vanishes and $F_n$ is the nearest whole number to $\varphi^n / \sqrt5$. The ratio $F_{n+1} / F_n$ tends to the golden ratio $\varphi$ for the same reason the lab's ellipse leans toward one eigenvector.


  
Markov chains. A column-stochastic matrix moves probability between states. Take

  
$$M = \begin{bmatrix}0.9 & 0.5\\ 0.1 & 0.5\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \lambda = 1,\; 0.4, \qquad \mathbf{v}_1 = \left(\tfrac{5}{6}, \tfrac{1}{6}\right)$$

  
The eigenvalue 1 never changes its part, and the $0.4^n$ part dies out. So from any start, $M^n \mathbf{x}$ settles at the steady state $(5/6, 1/6)$, and the distance to it shrinks by a factor of $0.4$ each step.


  
Differential equations. The same trick works for any function given by a power series. For the matrix exponential, $e^{At} = P e^{Dt} P^{-1}$, where $e^{Dt}$ just exponentiates each diagonal entry. So $\mathbf{x}' = A\mathbf{x}$ has the solution

  
$$\mathbf{x}(t) = c_1 e^{\lambda_1 t}\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2 e^{\lambda_2 t}\mathbf{v}_2,$$

  
with $c_1, c_2$ the eigen coordinates of $\mathbf{x}(0)$. Each eigen direction grows or decays on its own clock. The system settles to zero exactly when every eigenvalue has a negative real part.






  
7. When it fails

  

    
CaseExampleWhat goes wrong

    
      
Defective$\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$Eigenvalue $1$ is repeated, but there is only one eigen direction. Too few eigenvectors for $P$.

      
Complex eigenvalues$\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$No real eigenvectors. Over the complex numbers it does diagonalize, with $\pm i$.

    
  

  
A defective matrix can still be brought to a Jordan form $A = PJP^{-1}$, which is as close to diagonal as possible: the eigenvalues on the diagonal and some 1s just above it. For a $2 \times 2$ block,

  
$$J = \begin{bmatrix}\lambda & 1\\ 0 & \lambda\end{bmatrix}, \qquad J^n = \begin{bmatrix}\lambda^n & n\lambda^{n-1}\\ 0 & \lambda^n\end{bmatrix}$$

  
The extra $n\lambda^{n-1}$ term is the fingerprint of a missing eigenvector. For the shear, $\lambda = 1$, so $J^n = \begin{bmatrix}1 & n\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ grows linearly instead of staying put. The shear preset shows it: the ellipse stays tilted and keeps sliding, without settling on a second axis.






  
8. The best case: symmetric matrices

  
The spectral theorem says that a real symmetric matrix always has real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors. Normalize those to length 1 and $P$ becomes orthogonal, so $P^{-1} = P^{\mathsf T}$:

  
$$A = Q \Lambda Q^{\mathsf T}, \qquad Q^{\mathsf T} Q = I$$

  
No inversion is needed, and the numbers stay stable. Expanding the product gives a second way to read it, as a sum of projections onto the eigen directions:

  
$$A = \lambda_1 \mathbf{q}_1\mathbf{q}_1^{\mathsf T} + \lambda_2 \mathbf{q}_2\mathbf{q}_2^{\mathsf T} + \cdots + \lambda_n \mathbf{q}_n\mathbf{q}_n^{\mathsf T}$$

  
Keep only the terms with the largest $|\lambda_i|$ and you get the best low-rank approximation of $A$. Principal component analysis is this theorem applied to a covariance matrix. Try the Symmetric preset: the two dashed lines cross at a right angle.

$P$ is not unique. You can scale any column by a nonzero number, or reorder the columns as long as you reorder the diagonal of $D$ the same way. $D$ is unique up to that ordering.






  
10. The same thing in code

  
function diagonalize2([[a, b], [c, d]]) {
  if (b === 0 && c === 0) return { P: [[1, 0], [0, 1]], D: [a, d] };   // already diagonal
  const tr = a + d, disc = (a - d) ** 2 + 4 * b * c;            // tr² − 4·det
  if (disc < 0) throw new Error('complex eigenvalues: no real P');
  if (disc === 0) throw new Error('repeated eigenvalue, one eigenvector: defective');
  const s = Math.sqrt(disc), l1 = (tr + s) / 2, l2 = (tr - s) / 2;
  const vec = l => (b !== 0 ? [b, l - a] : [l - d, c]);         // solves (A − λI)v = 0
  const p = vec(l1), q = vec(l2);
  return { P: [[p[0], q[0]], [p[1], q[1]]], D: [l1, l2] };      // eigenvectors are columns
}

function power2(A, n) {
  const { P, D } = diagonalize2(A);
  const det = P[0][0] * P[1][1] - P[0][1] * P[1][0];
  const Pi = [[P[1][1] / det, -P[0][1] / det], [-P[1][0] / det, P[0][0] / det]];
  const Dn = D.map(l => l ** n);                                  // the only "power" we take
  return [0, 1].map(i => [0, 1].map(j =>
    P[i][0] * Dn[0] * Pi[0][j] + P[i][1] * Dn[1] * Pi[1][j]));         // P · Dⁿ · P⁻¹
}

diagonalize2([[4, 1], [2, 3]]);   // P = [[1, 1], [1, -2]], D = [5, 2]
power2([[4, 1], [2, 3]], 3);    // [[86, 39], [78, 47]], up to rounding

  
In practice you call a library. NumPy's np.linalg.eig(A) returns the eigenvalues and a matrix whose columns are unit eigenvectors. For symmetric matrices use np.linalg.eigh, which is faster, always returns real eigenvalues and gives an orthonormal $Q$. On a nearly defective matrix the columns of $P$ become nearly parallel, $P^{-1}$ blows up, and $PD^nP^{-1}$ loses accuracy. Check the condition number of $P$ before you trust the result.






  
11. Common mistakes

  
    
    
  • Putting the eigenvectors in the rows of $P$. They go in the columns.
    • 
    
  • Listing the eigenvalues in $D$ in a different order from the eigenvectors in $P$. Column $i$ of $P$ must match entry $i$ of $D$.
    • 
    
  • Swapping the sides: with eigenvectors in $P$, it is $A = PDP^{-1}$ and $D = P^{-1}AP$, not $A = P^{-1}DP$.
    • 
    
  • Thinking a repeated eigenvalue rules diagonalization out. The identity has one eigenvalue repeated $n$ times and is diagonal already. Count eigenvectors, not eigenvalues.
    • 
    
  • Mixing up diagonalizable and invertible. $\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$ is diagonal but not invertible. The shear is invertible but not diagonalizable.
    • 
    
  • Expecting perpendicular eigenvectors for every matrix. Only symmetric matrices (and, over $\mathbb{C}$, normal ones) are guaranteed an orthonormal eigenbasis.
    • 
  






  
12. Where it shows up

  

    
Dynamics
$A^n$
Repeated application of a rule, like population models and Markov chains, reduces to $\lambda^n$.

    
Differential equations
$e^{At}$
Linear systems split into independent modes, one per eigenvalue.

    
Data
PCA
Diagonalizing a covariance matrix finds the axes of greatest variance.

    
Graphs
PageRank
The ranking is the eigenvector for eigenvalue 1 of the link matrix, found by the power method.

    
Vibrations
modes
A bridge or a molecule vibrates as a mix of normal modes: eigenvectors, with eigenvalues setting the frequencies.

    
Recurrences
$F_n$
Any linear recurrence gets a closed formula, as with Fibonacci and the golden ratio.
