Matrix Diagonalization

Pick a basis made of eigenvectors, and a matrix turns into a plain list of stretch factors. Powers, exponentials and recurrences then become one line of arithmetic, and the long-run behavior of a system can be read straight off the diagonal.

Diagonalizable?

    

  






  
1. The idea

  
A square matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if it is similar to a diagonal matrix. That means there is an invertible $P$ and a diagonal $D$ with:

  
$$A = P D P^{-1} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad D = P^{-1} A P$$

  
The columns of $P$ are eigenvectors of $A$, and the diagonal of $D$ holds the matching eigenvalues, in the same order. Why: $AP = PD$ says, column by column, $A\mathbf{v}_i = \lambda_i\mathbf{v}_i$. That is the eigenvector equation $n$ times at once.

  
Geometrically it is a change of basis. The map $A$ does not change; only the coordinates used to describe it do. In the standard basis it shears and turns things. In the eigenvector basis the very same map is "scale coordinate 1 by $\lambda_1$, coordinate 2 by $\lambda_2$". Fig 1 makes this visible: the second and third panels are the only place where the map acts, and there it is a pure axis stretch.

  
$A$ is diagonalizable exactly when it has $n$ independent eigenvectors.






  
2. How to do it

  
    
    
  1. Solve $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ to get the eigenvalues.
    2. 
    
  2. For each eigenvalue, solve $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ to get a basis of its eigenspace.
    3. 
    
  3. If you collected $n$ independent eigenvectors in total, put them in the columns of $P$ and the eigenvalues, in the same order, on the diagonal of $D$. Otherwise $A$ is not diagonalizable.
    4. 
  

  
Worked example

  
$$A = \begin{bmatrix}4 & 1\\ 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}: \quad \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \lambda = 5,\; 2$$

  
For $\lambda = 5$: $(A - 5I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ gives $\mathbf{v} = (1, 1)$. For $\lambda = 2$: $\mathbf{v} = (1, -2)$. So

  
$$P = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & -2\end{bmatrix},\quad D = \begin{bmatrix}5 & 0\\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix},\quad P^{-1} = \frac{1}{-3}\begin{bmatrix}-2 & -1\\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{2}{3} & \tfrac{1}{3}\\ \tfrac{1}{3} & -\tfrac{1}{3}\end{bmatrix}$$

  
Check by multiplying back: $PD = \begin{bmatrix}5 & 2\\ 5 & -4\end{bmatrix}$, and $PD\,P^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix}\frac{10}{3} + \frac{2}{3} & \frac{5}{3} - \frac{2}{3}\\ \frac{10}{3} - \frac{4}{3} & \frac{5}{3} + \frac{4}{3}\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4 & 1\\ 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}$.

  
The choice is not unique. Scaling an eigenvector, or listing the eigenvalues in another order (with the columns of $P$ reordered to match), gives a different $P$ and $D$ for the same $A$.

  
If two eigenvalues are different, their eigenvectors are automatically independent. So $n$ distinct eigenvalues always give a diagonalizable matrix. Repeated eigenvalues need a check, see section 6.






  
3. Why it pays off: powers

  
The middle factors cancel when you multiply $A$ by itself:

  
$$A^n = (PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1})\cdots(PDP^{-1}) = P D^n P^{-1}, \qquad D^n = \begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1^n & 0\\ 0 & \lambda_2^n\end{bmatrix}$$

  
For the worked example that gives a closed form for every power at once:

  
$$A^n = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2\cdot 5^n + 2^n & 5^n - 2^n \\ 2\cdot 5^n - 2\cdot 2^n & 5^n + 2\cdot 2^n \end{bmatrix}$$

  
Put $n = 1$ and you get $A$ back. Put $n = 10$ and you get $\begin{bmatrix} 6510758 & 3254867 \\ 6509734 & 3255891 \end{bmatrix}$ without a single matrix product. As $n$ grows, the $5^n$ terms swamp the $2^n$ terms, every column becomes a multiple of $(1, 1)$, and the matrix is nearly rank one. That is the squeeze you see in the last panel of Fig 1.

  
The same identity also defines things like $A^{1/2}$ or $A^{-1}$: take the power of each eigenvalue. $A^{-1} = P D^{-1} P^{-1}$ exists exactly when no $\lambda_i$ is zero.






  
4. Dynamics: reading the future off the diagonal

  
Many systems advance by one fixed matrix per step: $\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = A\mathbf{x}_k$. Write the start in eigen coordinates, $\mathbf{x}_0 = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2$, and the whole future is

  
$$\mathbf{x}_k = A^k\mathbf{x}_0 = c_1\lambda_1^k\,\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\lambda_2^k\,\mathbf{v}_2$$

  
Each eigen component evolves on its own, as a geometric sequence. Pick a system below and drag the step count.


  

  
A Markov chain

  
Customers switch between two brands each month: 90% of brand 1's customers stay and 10% leave; 80% of brand 2's stay and 20% leave. With shares $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2)$, one month is

  
$$\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \begin{bmatrix}0.9 & 0.2\\ 0.1 & 0.8\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_k, \qquad \lambda_1 = 1,\ \mathbf{v}_1 = (2, 1), \qquad \lambda_2 = 0.7,\ \mathbf{v}_2 = (1, -1)$$

  
Start with everyone at brand 2, $\mathbf{x}_0 = (0, 1) = \frac{1}{3}(2, 1) - \frac{2}{3}(1, -1)$. Then $\mathbf{x}_k = \frac{1}{3}(2, 1) - \frac{2}{3}(0.7)^k(1, -1)$. The second term dies away, and the market settles at shares $\left(\frac{2}{3}, \frac{1}{3}\right)$, whatever the start. Every Markov matrix has eigenvalue $1$, and its eigenvector is the steady state.

  
Fibonacci

  
$\begin{bmatrix}F_{k+1}\\ F_k\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}^k\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}$. The eigenvalues are $\varphi = \frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}$ and $\psi = \frac{1-\sqrt5}{2}$, and diagonalizing gives Binet's formula $F_k = \frac{\varphi^k - \psi^k}{\sqrt5}$. Load the "Fibonacci" preset in Fig 1: by $n = 6$ the result already lies along $(\varphi, 1)$, and the sign of the small eigenvalue $\psi \lt 0$ makes the thin side flip back and forth.






  
5. Functions of a matrix

  
Anything defined by a power series can be applied through the diagonal. The most important is the matrix exponential:

  
$$e^{At} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(At)^k}{k!} = P\,\begin{bmatrix}e^{\lambda_1 t} & 0\\ 0 & e^{\lambda_2 t}\end{bmatrix}P^{-1}$$

  
It solves the linear system $\mathbf{x}'(t) = A\mathbf{x}(t)$ as $\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{At}\mathbf{x}(0)$. For example, two connected tanks with $A = \begin{bmatrix}-2 & 1\\ 1 & -2\end{bmatrix}$ have $\lambda = -1$ with $\mathbf{v} = (1, 1)$ and $\lambda = -3$ with $\mathbf{v} = (1, -1)$:

  
$$\mathbf{x}(t) = c_1 e^{-t}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix} + c_2 e^{-3t}\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}$$

  
The difference between the tanks (the $(1, -1)$ mode) fades three times faster than the total. In continuous time, the real parts of the eigenvalues play the role that $|\lambda|$ plays for steps.






  
6. When it fails

  

    
CaseExampleWhat goes wrong

    
      
Defective$\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$Eigenvalue $1$ is repeated, but there is only one eigen direction. Too few eigenvectors to fill $P$.

      
Complex eigenvalues$\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$No real eigenvectors. Over the complex numbers it does diagonalize, with $\lambda = \pm i$.

    
  

  
For each eigenvalue compare two counts. The algebraic multiplicity is how many times $\lambda$ is a root of the characteristic polynomial. The geometric multiplicity is the dimension of its eigenspace. $A$ is diagonalizable exactly when the two agree for every eigenvalue. The identity matrix passes ($\lambda = 1$ twice, eigenspace the whole plane); the shear fails ($\lambda = 1$ twice, eigenspace a line).

  
A defective matrix can still be brought to Jordan form, which is diagonal except for some 1s just above the diagonal. Its powers betray it: the shear has $S^n = \begin{bmatrix}1 & n\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$, which grows linearly in $n$ even though its only eigenvalue is $1$. Pure geometric sequences cannot produce that, which is another way to see that no eigenbasis exists. Try "Defective" in Fig 2: the orbit creeps along the eigen line at a steady pace instead of settling.

  
Complex eigenvalues, kept real

  
A real matrix with eigenvalues $\alpha \pm i\beta$ can be written as $P\begin{bmatrix}\alpha & -\beta\\ \beta & \alpha\end{bmatrix}P^{-1}$ with a real $P$. The middle block is a rotation by $\arg\lambda$ scaled by $|\lambda|$. So in a suitable basis the map is "turn and scale", which is the spiral in Fig 2.






  
7. The best case: symmetric matrices

  
The spectral theorem says that a real symmetric matrix always has real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors, so it is always diagonalizable. Normalize the eigenvectors to length 1 and $P$ becomes orthogonal, so $P^{-1} = P^{\mathsf T}$:

  
$$A = Q \Lambda Q^{\mathsf T} = \lambda_1\mathbf{q}_1\mathbf{q}_1^{\mathsf T} + \cdots + \lambda_n\mathbf{q}_n\mathbf{q}_n^{\mathsf T}$$

  
The second form writes $A$ as a weighted sum of projections onto perpendicular lines. For the symmetric preset:

  
$$\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1\\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix} = 3\cdot\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix} + 1\cdot\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1 & -1\\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}$$

  
No inversion is needed, and the computation is numerically stable. Principal component analysis is this theorem applied to a covariance matrix: keep the terms with the largest $\lambda$ and you have the best low-dimensional summary of the data.






  
8. What similar matrices share

  
$A$ and $D = P^{-1}AP$ describe the same map in different coordinates, so anything that does not depend on coordinates is the same for both.

  

    
Shared by similar matricesFor a diagonalizable $A$

    
      
Eigenvalues and characteristic polynomialthe diagonal of $D$

      
Trace$\lambda_1 + \cdots + \lambda_n$

      
Determinant$\lambda_1 \cdots \lambda_n$

      
Ranknumber of nonzero $\lambda_i$

    
  

  
Eigenvectors are not shared: they get transformed by $P$. And two matrices with the same eigenvalues need not be similar, as the identity and the shear show.






  
9. The same thing in code

  
// A^n for a diagonalizable 2x2 with real, distinct eigenvalues
function powDiag([[a, b], [c, d]], n) {
  const tr = a + d, det = a * d - b * c, s = Math.sqrt(tr * tr - 4 * det);
  const l1 = (tr + s) / 2, l2 = (tr - s) / 2;
  // eigenvectors from the first row of A - λI (assumes b ≠ 0)
  const P = [[b, b], [l1 - a, l2 - a]];
  const dP = P[0][0] * P[1][1] - P[0][1] * P[1][0];
  const Pi = [[P[1][1] / dP, -P[0][1] / dP], [-P[1][0] / dP, P[0][0] / dP]];
  const Dn = [l1 ** n, l2 ** n];
  return [0, 1].map(i => [0, 1].map(j =>
    P[i][0] * Dn[0] * Pi[0][j] + P[i][1] * Dn[1] * Pi[1][j]));
}

powDiag([[4, 1], [2, 3]], 10);
// [[6510758, 3254867], [6509734, 3255891]]  (up to rounding)

  
In practice, use numpy.linalg.eig for general matrices and numpy.linalg.eigh for symmetric ones. When $P$ is close to singular (eigenvectors nearly parallel), $P^{-1}$ amplifies rounding errors badly. Then repeated squaring, $A^{2k} = (A^k)^2$, which needs only about $\log_2 n$ products, is the safer way to compute powers.






  
10. Common mistakes

  
    
    
  • Mismatched order. Column $i$ of $P$ must be the eigenvector for the $i$-th diagonal entry of $D$.
    • 
    
  • Writing $A = P^{-1}DP$ with $P$ built from eigenvectors. With eigenvectors as columns, it is $A = PDP^{-1}$.
    • 
    
  • Thinking distinct eigenvalues are required. They are sufficient, not necessary: $I$ is diagonal already.
    • 
    
  • Confusing diagonalizable with invertible. $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ is diagonal but not invertible; the shear is invertible but not diagonalizable.
    • 
    
  • Assuming every real matrix diagonalizes over the reals. Rotations need complex numbers.
    • 
  






  
11. Where it shows up

  

    
Dynamics
$A^n$
Population models, Markov chains and recurrences reduce to $\lambda^n$ along each eigenvector.

    
Differential equations
$e^{At}$
Linear systems split into independent modes $e^{\lambda t}\mathbf{v}$, one per eigenvalue.

    
Data
PCA
Diagonalizing a covariance matrix finds the axes of greatest variance.

    
Vibration
normal modes
Coupled springs decouple in the eigenbasis into independent oscillators.

    
Quantum mechanics
energy basis
Diagonalizing the Hamiltonian gives the stationary states and their energies.

    
Graphs
random walks
The spectral gap $1 - |\lambda_2|$ of a walk's matrix says how fast it mixes.
