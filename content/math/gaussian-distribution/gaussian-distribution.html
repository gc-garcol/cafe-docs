Gaussian Distribution

The bell curve. Heights, measurement errors and noise in a signal all pile up around an average and thin out on both sides. Two numbers, the center and the spread, describe the whole shape.

1. The shape

  
A Gaussian, also called a normal distribution, is symmetric. Most values sit near the center, and the chance of a value falls off quickly as you move away from it. The center $\mu$ is the mean. The spread $\sigma$ is the standard deviation. A small $\sigma$ gives a tall, narrow bell. A large $\sigma$ gives a low, wide one. The area under the curve is always 1, so a taller peak means a narrower curve.

  
Two numbers fix the whole curve: where it is centered and how wide it is.






  
2. The formula

  
The height of the curve at $x$ is the probability density.

  
$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$

  
Read it from the inside out. The term $(x-\mu)^2$ is the squared distance from the center. Dividing by $2\sigma^2$ measures that distance in units of spread. The exponential turns a growing distance into a fast shrinking height. The factor in front, $1/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})$, is exactly what makes the total area equal to 1. The constant $\sqrt{2\pi}$ comes from the integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2}\,dx = \sqrt{2\pi}$, which you can compute with a clever switch to polar coordinates.

  
A density is not a probability. The chance of landing in an interval is the area under the curve over that interval:

  
$$P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$$

  
This integral has no closed form in elementary functions. It is written with the error function, $\operatorname{erf}$, and computed numerically. See Integrals for the idea of area as a sum.






  
3. The standard normal and z-scores

  
Any Gaussian can be turned into the standard one, with mean 0 and spread 1, by measuring how many standard deviations a value is from the mean:

  
$$z = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$$

  
That is why one table, or one function, is enough for every normal curve. A score of $z = 2$ always means "two standard deviations above the mean", whatever the units. In terms of the standard normal, the probability that $X$ is within $k$ standard deviations of the mean is:

  
$$P(\mu - k\sigma \le X \le \mu + k\sigma) = \operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt{2}}\right)$$

5. Why it appears everywhere

  
The central limit theorem says that the sum of many small independent effects is close to Gaussian, even if each effect is not. The lab shows it. Switch the source to One uniform and draw samples: the histogram is flat. Switch to Sum of 12 and the same kind of random number, added up, forms a bell.

  
$$\frac{S_n - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} \;\longrightarrow\; \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty$$

  
Here $S_n$ is the sum of $n$ independent draws with mean $\mu$ and spread $\sigma$. A person's height, the error in a measurement, and the noise on a wire are all made of many small pushes, so they look Gaussian. The sum of 12 uniforms in the lab is already close, since 12 uniform values on $[0,1]$ add to a mean of 6 and a variance of exactly 1.






  
6. The same thing in code

  
function pdf(x, mu, sigma) {
  const z = (x - mu) / sigma;
  return Math.exp(-0.5 * z * z) / (sigma * Math.sqrt(2 * Math.PI));
}

// Box-Muller: two uniform numbers make one Gaussian sample
function sample(mu, sigma) {
  const u = 1 - Math.random(), v = Math.random();
  return mu + sigma * Math.sqrt(-2 * Math.log(u)) * Math.cos(2 * Math.PI * v);
}






  
7. Where it shows up

  

    
Statistics
errors
Measurement noise, confidence intervals and many hypothesis tests assume a bell curve.

    
Machine learning
noise
Weight initialization, Gaussian processes, and diffusion models all start from Gaussian noise.

    
Signals
blur
A Gaussian filter smooths an image. Its Fourier transform is another Gaussian.

  

  
The Gaussian is also a common prior and likelihood in Bayes' Theorem, and its own Fourier transform keeps the same shape, see Fourier Transform. The bell is only as good as the data: heavy tailed things such as incomes or earthquake sizes are not Gaussian, and the 68–95–99.7 rule will under-count their extremes.
