L'Hôpital's Rule

When plugging in gives $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$, the limit is not decided yet. L'Hôpital's rule settles it by comparing how fast the top and the bottom change.

#lhopital#indeterminate form#limit#derivative

Try it first

Pick a quotient $\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ where both $f$ and $g$ pass through zero at $x = 0$. Slide $x$ toward 0. The two vertical bars are $f(x)$ and $g(x)$; their ratio is the quotient. Compare it with the ratio of slopes $\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$.

f (top) g (bottom) tangent at 0

1. The rule

Suppose $f$ and $g$ are differentiable near $a$, $g'(x) \ne 0$ near $a$ (except possibly at $a$), and the quotient has one of the two indeterminate forms:

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \lim_{x \to a} g(x) = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \pm\infty,\ \lim_{x \to a} g(x) = \pm\infty$$

If the limit of the ratio of derivatives exists (or is $\pm\infty$), then the original limit equals it:

$$\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$$

The same statement holds for one-sided limits ($x \to a^-$, $x \to a^+$) and for $x \to \pm\infty$. If the new quotient is still $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$, apply the rule again.

Differentiate the top and the bottom separately. This is not the quotient rule.

2. Why it works

Zoom in on $a$. A differentiable function looks like its tangent line there. Since $f(a) = g(a) = 0$, both tangent lines pass through $(a, 0)$:

$$f(x) \approx f'(a)\,(x - a), \qquad g(x) \approx g'(a)\,(x - a)$$

The common factor $(x - a)$ cancels, so the ratio of heights becomes the ratio of slopes:

$$\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \approx \frac{f'(a)\,(x - a)}{g'(a)\,(x - a)} = \frac{f'(a)}{g'(a)}$$

Fig 2 · Near a, the heights scale with the distance from a, so only the slopes survive in the ratio.

The full proof uses Cauchy's mean value theorem. For $x$ near $a$ there is a point $c$ between $a$ and $x$ with

$$\frac{f(x) - f(a)}{g(x) - g(a)} = \frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}$$

With $f(a) = g(a) = 0$ the left side is just $\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$. As $x \to a$, the point $c$ is squeezed toward $a$ too, so the two limits agree. This version does not need $f'(a)$ or $g'(a)$ to exist, only the limit of $\frac{f'}{g'}$.

The fourth row needs two rounds. After one round, $\frac{\sin x}{2x}$ is still $\frac{0}{0}$, so you differentiate again. In the lab, pick $(1 - \cos x)/x^2$: both tangent lines at 0 are flat, which is the picture of "first derivatives are both zero, go one level deeper".

The last two rows give a growth ranking. Applying the rule $n$ times to $\frac{x^n}{e^x}$ leaves $\frac{n!}{e^x} \to 0$, so for large $x$:

$$\ln x \ll x^n \ll e^x$$

4. Other indeterminate forms

The rule only reads quotients. Five other forms are also undecided, and each can be rewritten as $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ first.

0 · ∞
flip one

Write $f g = \frac{f}{1/g}$ or $\frac{g}{1/f}$.

∞ − ∞
combine

Put both terms over a common denominator.

1^∞, 0^0, ∞^0
take logs

Set $y = f^g$, so $\ln y = g \ln f$, a $0 \cdot \infty$ form.

$0 \cdot \infty$: move $x$ to the bottom as $1/x$.

$$\lim_{x \to 0^+} x \ln x = \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\ln x}{1/x} = \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{1/x}{-1/x^2} = \lim_{x \to 0^+} (-x) = 0$$

$\infty - \infty$: combine, then apply the rule twice.

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \left( \frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{\sin x} \right) = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x - x}{x \sin x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos x - 1}{\sin x + x \cos x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{-\sin x}{2 \cos x - x \sin x} = 0$$

$1^\infty$: the classic limit that defines $e$. Take logs and substitute $t = 1/x$.

$$\ln \left(1 + \tfrac{1}{x}\right)^x = \frac{\ln(1 + t)}{t} \xrightarrow{\;t \to 0^+\;} \frac{1/(1+t)}{1} \to 1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \lim_{x \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right)^x = e$$

Always finish by undoing the log: the limit of $\ln y$ is 1, so the limit of $y$ is $e^1$.

5. Pitfalls

  • Check the form first. $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x + 1}{x + 2} = \frac{1}{2}$ by plugging in. Applying the rule anyway gives $\frac{1}{1} = 1$, which is wrong.
  • Not the quotient rule. Use $\frac{f'}{g'}$, never $\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)'$.
  • It only works one way. If $\lim \frac{f'}{g'}$ does not exist, the original limit may still exist. For $\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{x + \sin x}{x}$ the rule gives $1 + \cos x$, which oscillates. Dividing by $x$ instead gives $1 + \frac{\sin x}{x} \to 1$.
  • It can loop. For $\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{e^x + e^{-x}}{e^x - e^{-x}}$ each round swaps the top and the bottom. Divide by $e^x$ and the limit is $1$.
  • Circular proofs. Using the rule on $\frac{\sin x}{x}$ assumes $(\sin x)' = \cos x$, and that derivative is usually proved from this same limit. It is a fine check, not a proof.

6. Connection to Taylor series

Taylor series explain the rule and are often faster. Expand the top and the bottom around $a$ and keep the first nonzero term of each:

$$\frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} = \frac{\frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^4}{24} + \cdots}{x^2} = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{x^2}{24} + \cdots \to \frac{1}{2}$$

The number of rounds you need equals the order of the first nonzero term. The rule is named after Guillaume de l'Hôpital, who published it in 1696 in the first calculus textbook. The result itself came from his teacher, Johann Bernoulli.
