Complex Analysis

Calculus on the complex plane. Differentiable here means much more than on the real line: every such function turns tiny shapes into rotated, scaled copies of themselves.

Try it first

A complex function f sends each point z in the left plane to a point w = f(z) in the right plane. The grid lines on the left bend into curves on the right, yet they still cross at right angles. Move the point and read how f′(z) rotates and scales its neighborhood.

1. Numbers with two parts

A complex number is z = x + iy with i² = −1. It lives at the point (x, y) of a plane. In polar form it has a length and an angle.

z = r·eiθ = r(cos θ + i sin θ)

Multiplying two complex numbers multiplies their lengths and adds their angles. So multiplication by z is a rotation plus a stretch. That single fact explains most of what follows.

2. Functions as maps

A real function draws a graph. A complex function needs four dimensions, two in and two out, so we picture it as a map between two planes, as in Fig 1.

  • z² squares the length and doubles the angle. It folds the plane around the origin.
  • 1/z inverts the length and flips the angle. It swaps the inside and outside of the unit circle.
  • eᶻ = eˣ(cos y + i sin y) turns horizontal lines into rays and vertical lines into circles.
  • sin z repeats along the real axis and grows quickly in the imaginary direction.

3. The complex derivative

The definition looks like the real one.

f′(z) = limh→0 [f(z + h) − f(z)] / h

The catch is that h is a complex number and may approach zero from any direction in the plane. The limit must agree along every path. That is a strong demand, and it forces the function to behave like multiplication by one complex number, f′(z), near each point.

Reading f′(z): its length is the local zoom, its angle is the local turn.

Try it in the lab. For z² at z = 0.8 + 0.6i the derivative is 2z = 1.6 + 1.2i. Its length is 2 and the readout reports that scale factor.

4. Cauchy–Riemann and analytic functions

Write f = u + iv. The limit agrees along the horizontal and vertical directions only when these two equations hold.

∂u/∂x = ∂v/∂y    and    ∂u/∂y = −∂v/∂x

A function that has a complex derivative on an open region is called analytic (or holomorphic). Because the derivative is a rotation and a stretch, analytic maps are conformal: they keep angles. That is why the grid in Fig 1 keeps its right-angle crossings, except at points where f′ = 0 or where f blows up.

Analytic functions are also far more rigid than real differentiable ones. Having one derivative means having all of them, and the function is fixed everywhere by its values on a tiny patch.

5. Integrating along a path

A contour integral adds up f(z) dz along a curve C in the plane.

∮C f(z) dz

Cauchy's theorem: if f is analytic everywhere inside and on a closed loop C, the integral around the loop is 0. The value depends only on the endpoints, so any path between them gives the same answer.

The interesting case is a loop that encloses a point where f fails to be analytic.

∮|z|=1 (1 / z) dz = 2πi

The function 1/z has a pole at the origin. Walking once counterclockwise around it gives 2πi, no matter how large or small the loop is.

Fig 2 · Only poles inside the loop contribute. A pole outside changes nothing.

6. Residues

Near a pole, f expands as a series with negative powers of (z − a). The coefficient of (z − a)−1 is the residue. It is the only term that survives a trip around the loop.

∮C f(z) dz = 2πi · Σ Res(f, ak)

For a simple pole at a the residue is lim (z − a)·f(z). Example: f(z) = 1/(z² + 1) has poles at ±i. At z = i the residue is 1/(2i). This is how real integrals such as ∫ dx/(x² + 1) = π get solved: close the path in the upper half plane and collect one residue.
