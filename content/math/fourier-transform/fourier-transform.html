Fourier Transform

Any signal is a stack of simple waves. The Fourier transform tells you which frequencies are in the stack, how strong each one is, and where each one starts. It turns hard operations on signals into easy ones on their frequencies, and the FFT makes it fast enough to run inside every phone.

1. Time domain and frequency domain

A recording of a chord is one wiggly line: air pressure over time. That is the time domain. The same sound can be described as three notes, each with a pitch, a loudness and a starting point. That is the frequency domain. Both descriptions hold exactly the same information, and the Fourier transform converts between them in either direction without losing anything.

Same signal, two views: when it happens, and what it is made of.

Why bother? Because many problems are hard in one view and easy in the other. Removing a hum at 50 Hz from a recording is nearly impossible to do sample by sample, but in the frequency domain it is "set one bar to zero". Blurring an image, solving the heat equation and multiplying huge numbers all become simple multiplications after a Fourier transform.

2. Fourier series: building a periodic wave

A wave that repeats every $2\pi$ can be written as a sum of sines and cosines whose frequencies are whole multiples of the base frequency:

$$f(t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \big(a_k \cos kt + b_k \sin kt\big)$$

Each coefficient measures how much of that one wave the signal contains, by averaging the signal against it:

$$a_k = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(t)\cos kt\,dt, \qquad b_k = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(t)\sin kt\,dt$$

Worked example: the square wave

Take $f(t) = 1$ for $0 \lt t \lt \pi$ and $-1$ for $-\pi \lt t \lt 0$. It is odd, so every $a_k = 0$. For the sines:

$$b_k = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} \sin kt\,dt = \frac{2}{\pi k}\big(1 - \cos k\pi\big) = \begin{cases} \frac{4}{\pi k} & k \text{ odd} \\ 0 & k \text{ even} \end{cases}$$
$$\text{square}(t) = \frac{4}{\pi}\left[\sin t + \frac{\sin 3t}{3} + \frac{\sin 5t}{5} + \cdots\right]$$

Each term is one circle in Fig 1: term $k$ turns $k$ times as fast and is $\frac{1}{k}$ as large. Put $t = \frac{\pi}{2}$ and you get Leibniz's series, $1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \cdots = \frac{\pi}{4}$.

The Gibbs phenomenon

Near a jump the partial sums overshoot by about 9% of the jump size, however many terms you add. With 100 terms the square-wave sum still peaks at $1.179$ instead of $1$. The overshoot gets squeezed into a narrower and narrower spike, so the average error goes to zero, but the peak does not. Fig 1 reports the peak in the readout. This is the ringing you see around sharp edges in heavily compressed images.

3. Why the coefficients work: orthogonality

The formulas for $a_k$ and $b_k$ come from one fact. Over a full period, different sines and cosines cancel when multiplied:

$$\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \sin mt\,\sin nt\,dt = \begin{cases} \pi & m = n \\ 0 & m \ne n \end{cases} \qquad \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \sin mt\,\cos nt\,dt = 0$$

Multiply $f$ by $\sin kt$ and integrate: every term of the series vanishes except the $k$-th, which leaves $\pi b_k$. That is the same trick as finding coordinates in an orthogonal basis in the Vector Spaces guide, where each coordinate is a single dot product. Here the "vectors" are functions, the dot product is $\langle f, g \rangle = \int f g\,dt$, and the sines and cosines are an orthogonal basis.

The tidiest way to write it uses complex exponentials, which bundle a cosine and a sine with the same frequency (see Euler's Formula):

$$f(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} c_k\,e^{ikt}, \qquad c_k = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(t)\,e^{-ikt}\,dt$$

$|c_k|$ is the strength of frequency $k$ and $\arg c_k$ is its phase. Negative $k$ are the same circles turning the other way; for a real signal $c_{-k}$ is the complex conjugate of $c_k$.

4. The transform: winding a signal around a circle

A signal that does not repeat has no base frequency, so it needs every frequency, not just whole multiples. The sum becomes an integral:

$$F(\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\, e^{-2\pi i \xi t}\,dt \qquad\qquad f(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} F(\xi)\, e^{2\pi i \xi t}\,d\xi$$

The left formula has a picture. $e^{-2\pi i \xi t}$ walks around the unit circle $\xi$ times per second. Multiplying by $f(t)$ stretches the radius to the signal's value. So the integral wraps the graph of $f$ around the origin at winding speed $\xi$ and adds up where it lands, which is (up to a factor) its center of mass. When the winding speed matches a frequency in the signal, the bumps all land on the same side and the center of mass jumps away from zero. Otherwise they spread evenly and cancel.

The value at $\xi = 0$ is just the average of the signal, which is why the lab shows a big peak there: the wound signal is shifted up so it stays positive. Real transforms of a signal with zero average have no such peak.

5. Properties that do the work

Most uses of the transform come from a handful of rules. Write $f \leftrightarrow F$ for a pair.

RuleTime domainFrequency domain
Linearity$a f + b g$$a F + b G$
Time shift$f(t - t_0)$$e^{-2\pi i \xi t_0} F(\xi)$: same strengths, new phases
Modulation$e^{2\pi i \xi_0 t} f(t)$$F(\xi - \xi_0)$: the spectrum slides, which is how radio works
Scaling$f(at)$$\frac{1}{|a|} F\!\left(\frac{\xi}{a}\right)$: squeeze one, stretch the other
Derivative$f'(t)$$2\pi i \xi\, F(\xi)$: calculus becomes algebra
Convolution$(f * g)(t) = \int f(s) g(t - s)\,ds$$F(\xi)\,G(\xi)$
Parseval$\int |f|^2\,dt$$= \int |F|^2\,d\xi$: energy is the same in both views

The convolution rule is the star. Blurring, echo, smoothing and many filters are convolutions, which cost $O(N^2)$ directly. Transform both, multiply point by point, transform back, and it costs $O(N \log N)$. The derivative rule turns linear differential equations into polynomial equations: for the heat equation $u_t = u_{xx}$, each frequency simply decays like $e^{-4\pi^2 \xi^2 t}$, so high frequencies (sharp details) vanish first.

6. The uncertainty principle

The scaling rule has a famous consequence: a signal cannot be narrow in time and narrow in frequency at once. Squeeze a pulse to make it shorter and its spectrum spreads. Measure the spreads by their standard deviations and

$$\sigma_t\,\sigma_\xi \ge \frac{1}{4\pi}$$

with equality only for the Gaussian bell. A short click has a broad spectrum; a pure tone lasts forever. To tell two notes 1 Hz apart you must listen for about a second. In quantum mechanics position and momentum are a Fourier pair, and this inequality becomes Heisenberg's uncertainty principle.

7. The Discrete Fourier Transform

Computers hold $N$ samples, not a continuous curve. The DFT turns $N$ samples into $N$ complex frequency values:

$$X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\, e^{-2\pi i k n / N}, \qquad x[n] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X[k]\, e^{2\pi i k n / N}$$

Bin $k$ means "$k$ cycles across the whole window". With sample rate $f_s$ that is $k \cdot \frac{f_s}{N}$ hertz. Play with a sampled signal below: the bars are $|X[k]|$, and the filter keeps only the bins below the cutoff before transforming back.

Three things the lab shows

  • Mirror image. For real samples $X[N-k]$ is the conjugate of $X[k]$, so the right half of the bars repeats the left half. Only bins $0$ to $\frac{N}{2}$ carry new information.
  • Aliasing. The highest frequency the samples can represent is half the sample rate, the Nyquist frequency. Set $f_1 = 60$: those samples are identical to a wave with 4 cycles, so the peak appears at bin 4. That is why audio is sampled at 44.1 kHz, a bit more than twice the 20 kHz limit of hearing, and why the analog signal is low-pass filtered first.
  • Leakage. The DFT assumes the window repeats. With $f_1 = 4.5$ the wave does not complete a whole number of cycles, the ends do not meet, and the energy smears into neighboring bins. Multiplying by a smooth window (Hann, Hamming) before the transform reduces the smearing.

8. The Fast Fourier Transform

Computed straight from the formula, each of the $N$ outputs needs $N$ multiplications: $O(N^2)$. The Cooley–Tukey FFT splits the sum into even- and odd-numbered samples. Each half is itself a DFT of size $\frac{N}{2}$, and the two halves combine with one multiplication per output:

$$X[k] = E[k] + w^k\,O[k], \qquad X\!\left[k + \tfrac{N}{2}\right] = E[k] - w^k\,O[k], \qquad w = e^{-2\pi i / N}$$

Apply the split again inside each half, down to single samples. There are $\log_2 N$ levels, each doing $O(N)$ work, so the total is $O(N \log N)$. This is the same divide-and-conquer shape as merge sort.

One second of CD audio is 44,100 samples. Direct, that is about two billion operations; with the FFT, under a million. Gauss sketched the idea in 1805; Cooley and Tukey rediscovered it in 1965, and it is often listed among the most important algorithms of the century.

9. The same thing in code

// Recursive radix-2 FFT. x: array of [re, im], length a power of 2.
function fft(x) {
  const N = x.length;
  if (N === 1) return [x[0]];
  const E = fft(x.filter((_, i) => i % 2 === 0));
  const O = fft(x.filter((_, i) => i % 2 === 1));
  const X = new Array(N);
  for (let k = 0; k < N / 2; k++) {
    const a = -2 * Math.PI * k / N, w = [Math.cos(a), Math.sin(a)];
    const t = [w[0] * O[k][0] - w[1] * O[k][1],     // w^k · O[k]
               w[0] * O[k][1] + w[1] * O[k][0]];
    X[k]         = [E[k][0] + t[0], E[k][1] + t[1]];
    X[k + N / 2] = [E[k][0] - t[0], E[k][1] - t[1]];
  }
  return X;
}

// 16 samples of sin(3 cycles) + 0.5·cos(5 cycles)
const N = 16;
const x = [...Array(N)].map((_, n) =>
  [Math.sin(2 * Math.PI * 3 * n / N) + 0.5 * Math.cos(2 * Math.PI * 5 * n / N), 0]);
fft(x).map(([re, im]) => Math.hypot(re, im).toFixed(1));
// bins 3 and 13 → 8.0, bins 5 and 11 → 4.0, the rest 0.0
// |X[k]| = amplitude · N / 2, and each tone shows up twice (k and N − k)

In practice use a library: numpy.fft.rfft, FFTW, or the Web Audio AnalyserNode. They handle any length, real input (half the work) and cache-friendly ordering.

10. What it is used for

Filter
remove noise

Zero the frequencies you do not want, then invert. Equalizers, hum removal and noise gates work this way.

Compress
keep big bars

JPEG uses a cosine transform on 8×8 blocks; MP3 and AAC drop frequencies the ear will not miss.

Multiply
fast convolution

Convolution in time is plain multiplication in frequency. Multiplying million-digit numbers uses the same trick.

Imaging
MRI

An MRI scanner measures the 2D Fourier transform of the body directly; the image is its inverse.

Audio
spectrograms

FFTs of short overlapping windows show how frequencies change over time, as in speech recognition.

Finance
option pricing

Carr–Madan and COS methods price options by FFT of the characteristic function of returns.

11. Common mistakes

  • Reading bins above $\frac{N}{2}$ as high frequencies. For real signals they are the negative frequencies, a mirror of the lower half.
  • Forgetting the scale. A sine of amplitude $A$ gives $|X[k]| = \frac{AN}{2}$ in the unnormalized DFT; libraries differ on where they put the $\frac{1}{N}$.
  • Sampling too slowly. Anything above Nyquist does not disappear; it comes back as a fake lower frequency.
  • Trusting peak heights for frequencies between bins. Leakage spreads them out; use a window or zero-padding for a finer grid.
  • Throwing away the phase. Magnitude alone cannot rebuild a signal; swapping phases between two images swaps most of what you see.
