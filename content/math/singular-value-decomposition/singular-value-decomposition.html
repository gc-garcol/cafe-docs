Singular Value Decomposition

Any matrix, square or not, symmetric or not, can be written as a rotation, a stretch along the axes, and a second rotation. That factorization is the SVD. It powers PCA, least squares, low-rank compression and the stability checks behind every regression you will fit.

1. The factorization

For any real $m \times n$ matrix $A$ there are orthogonal matrices $U$ ($m \times m$) and $V$ ($n \times n$) and a diagonal $\Sigma$ with non-negative entries such that

$$A = U\Sigma V^\top = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i\, u_i v_i^\top, \qquad \sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0$$

The columns $v_i$ are the right singular vectors, the $u_i$ the left singular vectors and the $\sigma_i$ the singular values. The number of non-zero singular values $r$ is the rank of $A$. Each $v_i$ is sent to a stretched $u_i$:

$$A v_i = \sigma_i u_i, \qquad A^\top u_i = \sigma_i v_i$$

2. Orthogonality and projections

Orthogonal matrices keep lengths and angles: $Q^\top Q = I$, so $\lVert Qx\rVert = \lVert x\rVert$. That is why $V^\top$ and $U$ only rotate or reflect the circle. All the change of shape lives in $\Sigma$.

Orthonormal vectors also give clean projections. The projection of $x$ onto the span of orthonormal columns $Q$ is $QQ^\top x$. The SVD hands you orthonormal bases for the four fundamental subspaces at once: the first $r$ columns of $U$ span the column space of $A$, and the first $r$ columns of $V$ span its row space.

$$P_{\text{col}(A)} = U_r U_r^\top, \qquad P_{\text{row}(A)} = V_r V_r^\top$$

3. Link to eigenvalues

Multiply $A$ by its transpose and the outer rotation cancels:

$$A^\top A = V\Sigma^\top\Sigma V^\top, \qquad AA^\top = U\Sigma\Sigma^\top U^\top$$

So the $v_i$ are eigenvectors of $A^\top A$, the $u_i$ are eigenvectors of $AA^\top$, and $\sigma_i^2$ are their shared eigenvalues. For a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix, such as a covariance matrix, the SVD and the eigendecomposition are the same thing.

4. Low-rank approximation

Keep only the first $k$ terms of the sum. The Eckart-Young theorem says no rank-$k$ matrix is closer to $A$:

$$A_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top, \qquad \lVert A - A_k\rVert_F^2 = \sum_{i>k} \sigma_i^2$$

This is PCA. Center a returns matrix $X$ (days by assets), take its SVD, and the $v_i$ are the principal components, the factor loadings. The share of variance explained by the first $k$ is $\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_i^2 / \sum_i \sigma_i^2$. In equities the first component is usually the market and explains a large part of the total.

5. Least squares and the condition number

The least-squares solution of $X\beta \approx y$ uses the pseudo-inverse, which simply inverts the non-zero singular values:

$$\hat\beta = X^{+} y = V\Sigma^{+}U^\top y = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \frac{u_i^\top y}{\sigma_i}\, v_i$$

A tiny $\sigma_i$ divides the data by almost zero, so noise in $y$ is blown up along $v_i$. The condition number $\kappa = \sigma_1/\sigma_r$ measures this. Highly correlated features, which is multicollinearity, give large $\kappa$ and unstable coefficients. Ridge regression fixes it by replacing $1/\sigma_i$ with $\sigma_i/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda)$.

import numpy as np
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X - X.mean(0), full_matrices=False)
explained = s**2 / np.sum(s**2)          # variance share per component
X_k = U[:, :k] * s[:k] @ Vt[:k]          # best rank-k approximation
kappa = s[0] / s[-1]                     # condition number
beta = Vt.T @ ((U.T @ y) / s)            # least squares via SVD

Pitfalls

  • Singular vectors are defined only up to sign. Two libraries can return $u_i, v_i$ and $-u_i, -v_i$ for the same matrix, so do not compare loadings across runs without fixing the sign.
  • Center (and usually scale) the data before using the SVD as PCA, or the first component just points at the mean.
  • Use full_matrices=False for tall matrices. The full $U$ of a 10,000-day matrix is 10,000 by 10,000.
  • With repeated singular values the singular vectors are not unique. Any rotation inside that subspace works, so components can swap between samples.
