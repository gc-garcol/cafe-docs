Determinants

A single number attached to a square matrix. It says how much the matrix scales area or volume, whether it flips orientation, and whether it can be undone at all. One formula, three meanings, and a surprising number of uses.

1. Area scaling

A linear map keeps grid lines straight, parallel and evenly spaced. So every little grid square turns into the same parallelogram, and every shape, which is a pile of tiny squares, has its area multiplied by the same factor. That factor is $|\det A|$. In the lab the ellipse area is always $\pi r^2 \cdot |\det A|$, whatever you do.

In three dimensions the same number scales volume: the unit cube becomes a slanted box (a parallelepiped) with volume $|\det A|$. In $n$ dimensions it scales $n$-dimensional volume.

$\det A$ is the signed factor by which $A$ stretches area or volume.

2. Sign and orientation

Go counter-clockwise from $\mathbf{e}_1$ to $\mathbf{e}_2$. If $A\mathbf{e}_1$ to $A\mathbf{e}_2$ is still counter-clockwise, the determinant is positive. If it turned clockwise, the plane was mirrored and the determinant is negative. "Swap axes" is a pure mirror: area kept, orientation reversed, $\det = -1$.

The morph slider shows why the sign is not a detail. Moving continuously from $I$ (determinant $1$) to a matrix with negative determinant, the determinant is a continuous function of $t$, so it must pass through $0$. At that instant the plane is flattened. In three dimensions this is why your left hand can never be rotated into your right hand.

3. The $2 \times 2$ formula

$$\det\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} = ad - bc$$

This is the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by the columns $(a, c)$ and $(b, d)$. Here is why, for positive entries. Put the parallelogram in a box of size $(a + b) \times (c + d)$ and remove the corners.

If you drag the arrows until both columns lie on one line, the area is zero. A zero determinant means the matrix squashes the plane onto a line or a point. Information is lost, so no inverse exists. When $\det A \neq 0$, the inverse is $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}$, and its determinant is $\frac{1}{\det A}$.

4. Three rules define it

The determinant is the only function of the columns of an $n \times n$ matrix that obeys three rules. All the other properties, and every formula, follow from them.

RuleStatementArea picture
Normalized$\det I = 1$the unit square has area 1
Linear in each columnscaling a column by $c$ scales $\det$ by $c$; a column that is a sum splits the $\det$ into a sumstretch one side, the area stretches the same
Alternatingswapping two columns flips the sign; two equal columns give $0$reversing the order of the sides reverses orientation

One consequence: adding a multiple of one column to another changes nothing. Geometrically that is a shear, which slides the parallelogram along its base without changing its height. Try the "Shear" preset: the shape tilts, the determinant stays $1$.

5. Larger matrices: cofactor expansion

For bigger matrices, expand along any row or column. Each entry multiplies the determinant of the smaller matrix left after deleting its row and column (its minor), with signs from a checkerboard pattern:

$$\det A = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (-1)^{i+j}\, a_{ij}\, \det M_{ij} \qquad \begin{bmatrix} + & - & + \\ - & + & - \\ + & - & + \end{bmatrix}$$

Choose the row or column with the most zeros: each zero kills a whole term. For $3 \times 3$ matrices there is also the rule of Sarrus: add the three down-right diagonal products, subtract the three up-right ones. For $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ that is $(12 + 0 + 1) - (3 + 4 + 0) = 6$. Sarrus does not extend to $4 \times 4$.

6. How computers do it: row reduction

Cofactor expansion of an $n \times n$ matrix calls itself $n$ times on $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ matrices, which adds up to about $n!$ products. For $n = 20$ that is $2.4 \times 10^{18}$. Row reduction needs about $\frac{2}{3}n^3$, around $5{,}000$ operations for the same matrix.

The idea: use the rule "adding a multiple of one row to another changes nothing" to clear everything below the diagonal. A triangular matrix has determinant equal to the product of its diagonal, so the answer is the product of the pivots, with a sign flip for every row swap.

  1. Start with $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.
  2. Subtract $\frac{1}{2}$ row 1 from rows 2 and 3: rows become $(0, 3, 1.5)$ and $(0, 1, 1.5)$.
  3. Subtract $\frac{1}{3}$ row 2 from row 3: row 3 becomes $(0, 0, 1)$.
  4. Pivots $2, 3, 1$, no swaps: $\det = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 = 6$.

7. Rules that save work

RuleEffect on the determinant
Swap two rowschanges the sign
Multiply one row by $c$multiplies the determinant by $c$
Add a multiple of one row to anotherno change
Two equal or proportional rowsdeterminant is $0$
Scale the whole matrix$\det(cA) = c^n \det A$ for $n \times n$
Product$\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B$
Inverse$\det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det A}$
Transpose$\det A^{\mathsf T} = \det A$, so every row rule is also a column rule
Triangular or diagonalproduct of the diagonal entries
Eigenvalues$\det A = \lambda_1 \lambda_2 \cdots \lambda_n$

The product rule has a one-line geometric proof. $AB$ means "do $B$, then $A$". $B$ scales areas by $\det B$, then $A$ scales them by $\det A$, so together they scale by the product. It is remarkable because the determinant of a sum has no such rule: in general $\det(A + B) \ne \det A + \det B$.

8. What a zero means

For an $n \times n$ matrix these statements are all equivalent. If one holds, they all do.

  • $\det A \neq 0$
  • $A$ has an inverse
  • The columns are independent, so the rank is $n$
  • $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has exactly one solution for every $\mathbf{b}$
  • $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ has only the solution $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$
  • Zero is not an eigenvalue of $A$

Cramer's rule

Determinants also write down the solution of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ directly: $x_i = \frac{\det A_i}{\det A}$, where $A_i$ is $A$ with column $i$ replaced by $\mathbf{b}$. For $2x + y = 5$, $x - y = 1$:

$$x = \frac{\det\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}}{\det\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}} = \frac{-6}{-3} = 2, \qquad y = \frac{\det\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}{-3} = \frac{-3}{-3} = 1$$

It is neat for $2 \times 2$ and $3 \times 3$ systems and for proofs. For large systems it is far slower than elimination.

9. Three dimensions

For three vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}$ in space, the determinant of the matrix with those columns is the signed volume of the box they span. It equals the scalar triple product:

$$\det[\,\mathbf{u}\;\mathbf{v}\;\mathbf{w}\,] = \mathbf{u} \cdot (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w})$$

The sign follows the right-hand rule. The cross product itself is usually remembered as a determinant with the unit vectors in the top row:

$$\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w} = \det\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ v_1 & v_2 & v_3 \\ w_1 & w_2 & w_3 \end{bmatrix}$$

Four points lie on one plane exactly when the box spanned by three edges from one of them has zero volume.

10. The same thing in code

function det(M) {
  const A = M.map(r => r.slice()), n = A.length;
  let d = 1;
  for (let col = 0; col < n; col++) {
    // partial pivoting: use the largest entry for stability
    let p = col;
    for (let r = col + 1; r < n; r++) if (Math.abs(A[r][col]) > Math.abs(A[p][col])) p = r;
    if (A[p][col] === 0) return 0;
    if (p !== col) { [A[p], A[col]] = [A[col], A[p]]; d = -d; }  // swap flips the sign
    d *= A[col][col];
    for (let r = col + 1; r < n; r++) {
      const m = A[r][col] / A[col][col];
      for (let k = col; k < n; k++) A[r][k] -= m * A[col][k];
    }
  }
  return d;
}

det([[2, 0, 1], [1, 3, 2], [1, 1, 2]]);  // 6

In floating point, do not test "is this matrix singular?" with det(A) === 0, or even with a small threshold. The matrix $0.1 I$ of size $100 \times 100$ has determinant $10^{-100}$ and is perfectly well behaved. Use the rank or the condition number instead.

11. Common mistakes

  • $\det(A + B) = \det A + \det B$. False in general. Only products behave.
  • $\det(2A) = 2\det A$. For an $n \times n$ matrix it is $2^n \det A$: every row gets doubled.
  • Forgetting the checkerboard signs in cofactor expansion, especially along row 2 or column 2, which start with $-$.
  • Using Sarrus on a $4 \times 4$ matrix.
  • Talking about the determinant of a non-square matrix. It is not defined.

12. Where it shows up

Calculus
Jacobian

Changing variables in an integral scales by $|\det J|$. For polar coordinates $\det J = r$, which is where $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$ comes from.

Eigenvalues
$\det(A - \lambda I)$

Setting it to zero gives the characteristic polynomial, whose roots are the eigenvalues.

Geometry
orientation tests

The sign of a $2 \times 2$ determinant tells whether three points turn left or right. Convex hull and mesh code rely on it.

Statistics
Gaussian

The multivariate normal density has $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(2\pi\Sigma)}}$ in front, the volume correction for the covariance $\Sigma$.

Physics
volume flow

A flow that keeps $\det = 1$ preserves volume. Liouville's theorem says Hamiltonian mechanics does.

Graphics
mirrors

A negative model-matrix determinant flips triangle winding, so renderers swap back-face culling.
