Determinants

A single number attached to a square matrix. It says how much the matrix scales area or volume, whether it flips orientation, and whether it can be undone at all.

Try it first

The columns of the matrix are where the two unit vectors $e_1$ and $e_2$ land. The unit square becomes a parallelogram. Its area is $|\det A|$. Blue means orientation is kept. Orange means it is flipped. When the parallelogram collapses to a line, the determinant is zero.

1. Area scaling

  
Every linear map scales all areas by the same factor. That factor is the absolute value of the determinant. The sign records whether the plane was reflected: negative means a mirror image. In three dimensions the same number scales volume.

  
$\det A$ is the signed factor by which $A$ stretches area or volume.






  
2. The $2 \times 2$ formula

  
$$\det\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} = ad - bc$$

  
This is the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by the columns $(a, c)$ and $(b, d)$. If you drag the sliders until the two columns point along one line, the area is zero. A zero determinant means the matrix squashes the plane onto a line or a point. Information is lost, so no inverse exists.

  
When $\det A \neq 0$ the inverse is $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}$, and its determinant is $1/\det A$.






  
3. Larger matrices: cofactor expansion

  
For bigger matrices, expand along a row. Each entry multiplies the determinant of the smaller matrix left after deleting its row and column, with alternating signs:

  
$$\det A = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (-1)^{1+j}\, a_{1j}\, \det M_{1j}$$

  
Here $M_{1j}$ is $A$ without row 1 and column $j$. Try your own $3 \times 3$ matrix. The readout lists the three terms.


  





  
4. Rules that save work

  

    
RuleEffect on the determinant

    
      
Swap two rowsChanges the sign

      
Multiply one row by $c$Multiplies the determinant by $c$

      
Add a multiple of one row to anotherNo change

      
Two equal or proportional rowsDeterminant is $0$

      
Product$\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B$

      
Transpose$\det A^{\mathsf T} = \det A$

      
Triangular matrixProduct of the diagonal entries

    
  

  
The third rule is how computers do it. Row reduce to a triangular matrix, track the sign changes, and multiply the diagonal. That takes about $n^3$ steps, while cofactor expansion takes about $n!$.






  
5. What a zero means

  
For an $n \times n$ matrix these statements are all equivalent. If one holds, they all do.

  
    
    
  • $\det A \neq 0$
    • 
    
  • $A$ has an inverse
    • 
    
  • The columns are independent, so the rank is $n$
    • 
    
  • $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ has only the solution $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$
    • 
    
  • Zero is not an eigenvalue of $A$
    • 
  

  
Cramer's rule uses determinants to solve $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$: $x_i = \det A_i / \det A$, where $A_i$ has column $i$ replaced by $\mathbf{b}$. It is neat for small systems and slow for large ones.






  
6. Where it shows up

  

    
Calculus
Jacobian
Changing variables in an integral scales by the determinant of the Jacobian.

    
Geometry
volume
The volume of a parallelepiped is the determinant of its edge vectors.

    
Eigenvalues
$\det(A - \lambda I)$
The characteristic polynomial finds the eigenvalues of a matrix.
