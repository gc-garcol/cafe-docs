Polynomial Interpolation

Given a handful of points, find the one polynomial that passes through all of them. It is the simplest way to turn scattered data into a smooth formula you can evaluate anywhere.

1. The problem

You measured a quantity at a few inputs: $(x_0, y_0), (x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_n, y_n)$. You want a function $p$ with $p(x_i) = y_i$ for every $i$, so you can estimate values between the measurements.

Connect the dots with a smooth curve that can be written as a formula.

2. One and only one

Through $n+1$ points with distinct $x$ values there is exactly one polynomial of degree at most $n$. Two points give a line, three give a parabola, four give a cubic.

Why only one? Suppose two such polynomials existed. Their difference has degree at most $n$ but is zero at $n+1$ points. A nonzero polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ roots, so the difference must be zero everywhere.

The direct way to find it is to solve for the coefficients. Plugging each point into $p(x) = c_0 + c_1 x + \dots + c_n x^n$ gives a linear system with the Vandermonde matrix:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_0 & \cdots & x_0^n \\ 1 & x_1 & \cdots & x_1^n \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 1 & x_n & \cdots & x_n^n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \\ \vdots \\ c_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} y_0 \\ y_1 \\ \vdots \\ y_n \end{bmatrix}$$

This works but is slow, $O(n^3)$, and the matrix becomes badly conditioned as $n$ grows. The next two forms avoid it.

3. Lagrange form

Build one basis polynomial per point. Each one equals 1 at its own point and 0 at every other point.

$$\ell_j(x) = \prod_{k \ne j} \frac{x - x_k}{x_j - x_k}$$

Then weight each basis polynomial by the measured value and add them up. At $x = x_i$ only $\ell_i$ survives, so the sum returns $y_i$.

$$p(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{n} y_j\,\ell_j(x)$$

Worked example with points $(0,1)$, $(1,3)$, $(2,2)$:

$$p(x) = 1\cdot\frac{(x-1)(x-2)}{2} + 3\cdot\frac{x(x-2)}{-1} + 2\cdot\frac{x(x-1)}{2} = -\frac{3}{2}x^2 + \frac{7}{2}x + 1$$

Check: $p(0) = 1$, $p(1) = -1.5 + 3.5 + 1 = 3$, and $p(2) = -6 + 7 + 1 = 2$.

4. Newton form

Lagrange is clean but adding a point means starting over. Newton's form builds the polynomial one term at a time, so a new point only adds a new term.

$$p(x) = a_0 + a_1(x-x_0) + a_2(x-x_0)(x-x_1) + \cdots$$

The coefficients are divided differences, computed from a small table in $O(n^2)$ steps. Evaluating the result with nested multiplication costs $O(n)$ per point.

$$a_k = f[x_0, \dots, x_k], \qquad f[x_i, x_j] = \frac{f(x_j) - f(x_i)}{x_j - x_i}$$

The first divided difference is just the slope between two points, which links this idea back to derivatives.

5. How big is the error?

If the points sample a smooth function $f$, the gap between $f$ and the interpolating polynomial has a precise form. It looks like the Taylor remainder, with the factor $(x-a)^{n+1}$ replaced by a product over all nodes.

$$f(x) - p(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}\prod_{i=0}^{n}(x - x_i)$$

Here $\xi$ is some unknown point in the interval. Two things control the error. One is how fast the derivatives of $f$ grow. The other is the node product $\prod (x - x_i)$, which you can shape by choosing where to place the points.

6. Runge's phenomenon

More points do not always mean a better fit. For $f(x) = \frac{1}{1 + 25x^2}$ on $[-1, 1]$ with equally spaced nodes, the error near the ends grows without bound as $n$ increases. The node product is largest near the edges when points are evenly spread, and this function has large high-order derivatives.

The fix is to bunch the nodes toward the ends. Chebyshev nodes do this and minimize the largest value of the node product:

$$x_i = \cos\!\left(\frac{(2i+1)\pi}{2(n+1)}\right), \quad i = 0, \dots, n$$

Switch to Chebyshev in the lab and compare the max error. It drops steadily as points are added.

7. What people do in practice

  • Keep the degree low. Stitch many low-degree pieces instead of one high-degree polynomial. Piecewise linear and cubic splines are the usual choice for data.
  • Use stable evaluation. The barycentric form of the Lagrange formula evaluates in $O(n)$ per point and avoids the Vandermonde trouble. This page uses it.
  • Do not extrapolate. Outside the range of the points, a polynomial diverges fast.
  • Where it appears. Numerical integration rules such as Simpson's rule, root finding, Shamir's secret sharing, and error-correcting codes are all built on interpolation.
